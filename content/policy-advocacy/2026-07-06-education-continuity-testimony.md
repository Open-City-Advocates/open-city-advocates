**Before the Committee of the Whole**  
**Council of the District of Columbia**  
**Public Hearing on Bill 26-403, the "Education Continuity for Students in the Care of D.C. Amendment Act of 2025"**

**July 6, 2026**

**Testimony of Kyla Woods, MPM, Associate Director of Policy & Outreach, on behalf of Penelope Spain, Esq., Co-Founder & Chief Executive Officer**

Good morning, Chairman Mendelson and members of the Committee of the Whole. My name is Kyla Woods, and I am the Associate Director of Policy & Outreach at Open City Advocates. Our CEO, Penelope Spain, is inside a federal prison today, working with a client. She asked me to deliver our testimony on her behalf.

Open City Advocates provides legal representation and holistic mentoring to youth committed to DC's Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services. We begin working with our clients after they have been removed from their homes and placed in secure facilities, and we stay with them through reentry. Open City Advocates strongly supports Bill 26-403, and we thank Councilmember Parker for introducing it.

The Council has heard this from us before. In November 2020, Open City Advocates testified in support of this bill's predecessor. Six school years have passed. The ninth graders of that fall have finished school, or given up on it, waiting for a fix. The problem has not moved.

For more than two decades, we have watched what education does for our clients: it leads to jobs, cuts the risk of rearrest, and reconnects young people to their communities. One of our clients completed his GED after years of disillusionment with school and multiple DYRS placement disruptions. With that credential, he found the confidence to work three jobs and set a new goal: college. Credits matter for exactly this reason. Wendy Cavendish's study of committed youth found that the more credits a young person earns while confined, the more likely they are to return to school and earn a diploma after release.[^1]

Here is what we see instead. Nearly every DYRS placement change means a school change, and nearly every school change costs credits. Our clients are told to repeat courses they already passed. They, their families, and their advocates spend weeks chasing transcripts, school records, and IEP updates. And when young people learn that their hours in a facility classroom will not count toward graduation, they reasonably stop trying.

Bill 26-403 takes on each of these problems. Three provisions matter most to us:

- The transition team. An IEP-style team for credit transfer, so a student has an education plan in place before a placement changes.
- The uniform course catalogue. OSSE's catalogue and equivalency table mean DCPS, public charter schools, and non-public schools finally speak one language about which courses earn which credits toward graduation.
- Enrollment within two school days. No student should sit at home losing momentum while adults sort out paperwork.

One more provision matters to us: stackable partial credits. They mean the work a student does in any classroom counts somewhere, and keeps counting until a course is complete.

Chairman Mendelson, we know you want evidence that this bill is universally applicable. It is. Our clients are the stress test: they move more often, with less warning, and with more agencies involved than nearly any other students in the District. But the same structure serves students experiencing homelessness, students in military families, students in foster care, and any student whose family moves in the middle of a transcript. This is why we work alongside education advocates year after year: these problems come up for our clients again and again. A system that can move credits for a young person leaving a secure facility can move credits for anyone.

We understand many details will be worked out at markup. The core of the bill must survive it: transition planning, one course catalogue, fast enrollment, and credits that count. Our clients have waited six years for this. We urge the Committee to move it. Thank you, and I welcome any questions you or other Councilmembers may have.

[^1]: Wendy Cavendish, *Academic Attainment During Commitment and Postrelease Education-Related Outcomes of Juvenile Justice-Involved Youth With and Without Disabilities*, 22 Journal of Emotional and Behavioral Disorders 1, 41-52 (2014) (finding that an increase in the number of credits earned during confinement was related to an increase in the likelihood of returning to school and earning a diploma after release).
