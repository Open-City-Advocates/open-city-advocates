**YSC Overcrowding, Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services**  
**Committee on Youth Affairs, Council of the District of Columbia**  
**Councilmember Zachary Parker, Chairperson**

**October 16, 2025**

## Testimony of Joshua A. Miller, PhD, Research & Advocacy Director

Good afternoon. My name is Joshua Miller, I am the Research & Advocacy Director at Open City Advocates, which provides legal representation, holistic advocacy, and mentoring to youth who have been committed to the DC Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services or DYRS. I also participated in CCE’s Awaiting Placement working group.

Since the summer of 2024, I have been visiting with the Youth Council at YSC on a monthly basis. What I’ve observed resembles an episode of the anxiety-driven restaurant show, *The Bear* (starring Ayo Edebiri): orders pile up while the exhausted team works in constant crunch mode. The kitchen lacks the right ingredients, animosity festers among staff, and customers keep being seated long after the tables and chairs have run out. In the YSC “kitchen,” overcrowding is the result of failures both upstream in the community, and downstream in placement and procurement delays. It means the staff are dropping or ignoring policy, missing opportunities to fix things, and genuinely overwhelmed.

DYRS has been struggling with this kind of dysfunction and overcrowding for at least two years. DYRS staff consistently describe the agency as chaotic and mismanaged. Even during yesterday’s visit, we learned that policies had been suspended on crucial matters (like unit mixing) because the situation was so abnormal. Overcrowding leads to “beefs,” which lead to further separations, stay-aways, movement delays, and programming segregation. Staff and management lurch from crisis to crisis without resolution.

Some young people blame the Care Coordination team for placement delays, describing Care Coordinators as deceptive, coercive, and unhelpful. I spoke to one young woman who had been accepted to an out-of-state university but couldn't attend because a GPS monitoring violation left her sitting at YSC. I would posit that a university is a better placement than a jail.

One simple example illustrates this dysfunction. In March, young people at YSC told us they wanted a chess club. Georgetown’s Karla Talley immediately reached out to Black-led chess groups in the city, including the Big Chair Chess Club and Chocolate City Chess Club, and secured their interest in volunteering to provide “Think Before You Move” programming that has succeeded in youth justice settings. She forwarded this information to DYRS leadership on April 7th. Seven months later, the chess club still hasn’t begun, though it looks like it will start in November with a Halloween kickoff.

We’ve observed similar delays with other groups offering free services: WeFit for athletics, donated books from DC Public Library. Volunteer coordination is exhausting work, I understand, and DYRS may now have the right woman in the job. But these are inspiring community leaders offering transformative programming that could help young people reimagine themselves. It will take dozens of such programs to meet the need, yet the energy bubbling up across the city remains trapped on the outside looking in.

The stakes are far higher than at any restaurant. These are our District’s children being thrown away, not spoiled meals. But the challenge is the same: YSC needs to efficiently move young people to their next placement (ideally in the community). They need to serve customers and get them out the door. But instead DC continues to use the facility for long-term detention.

We have a roadmap for how to respond to this crisis. During COVID, these facilities prioritized decarceration. Young people were safely transitioned to community settings, and the system became more manageable. We need to beef up the continuum of care in the community, for sure, and I’m happy to discuss community-based alternatives to hardware-secure detention. But it’s time to treat overcrowding like the catastrophe it is, to use the same urgency we showed during the pandemic and take on those same emergency measures again.

It may be tempting to think that the solution to overcrowding is to build more beds. But this is like saying that we can solve problems in the kitchen by procuring more tables and chairs; I think what we learn from kitchen dramas like *The Bear* is that the solution to dysfunction is NOT to build a bigger restaurant.
