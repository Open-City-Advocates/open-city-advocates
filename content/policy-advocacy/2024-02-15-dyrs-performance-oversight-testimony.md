**Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services Performance Oversight Hearing**

**Committee on Recreation, Libraries, and Youth Affairs, Council of the District of Columbia**

**Chairman White and Members of the Committee**

**Thursday, February 15, 2024**

## Testimony of Penelope Spain, Chief Executive Officer

Good morning. My name is Penelope Spain. I am a resident of Ward 4 and for the past 19 years have served as CEO of Open City Advocates, which provides legal representation and holistic advocacy to youth who have been committed to the DC Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services (DYRS). We also engage in systemic reform efforts and participate in numerous working groups dedicated to supporting court-involved youth and returning citizens.

As you're aware, over the past 12 months, DYRS has been undergoing a leadership change during one of our city's most challenging periods in terms of public safety. At a time when our youth need us more than ever, DYRS is struggling in some of its most basic tasks. Leadership changes have meant delays in service contracts, pauses in agency improvement initiatives, and an ever-changing landscape where DYRS staff and community-based organizations are completely disjointed in their efforts to support youth and families. Children who DYRS has determined can safely be served in the community are languishing behind bars due to a lack of community-based placements and services. As of Monday of this week, there were 38 youth in the Youth Services Center in a holding pattern known as "awaiting placement" – those children have either been deemed not a risk to public safety and yet are locked in a non-therapeutic, traumatic, and inherently dangerous facility or they are in need of robust mental health services and are stuck in a facility where Director Abed previously testified they were prone to decompensate for lack of therapeutic services. And children at New Beginnings are faring only slightly better. They have access to fresh air, sunlight, and meaningful education services, yet as far as we know, nearly all vocational training and enrichment programs are on pause – their contracts expired at the end of the last fiscal year. Four and a half months later, DYRS still hasn't finalized and implemented new contracts. As for community-based reentry services for our children upon their release from New Beginnings, YSC, or out-of-state facilities? We haven't seen such a lack of quality, coordinated services in almost 20 years.

Today, I'd like to call to your attention two issues that particularly need your oversight.

First, to address a lack of community-based group homes, therapeutic foster homes, and a secure facility for girls, DYRS is currently in contract negotiations with locked residential facilities as far away as Georgia, Florida, and Idaho. We've been down this very dangerous road and I highly caution us against going down it again. Far away residentials rarely offer school credits that transfer back to the District, which means children get pulled behind in their education instead of moving towards graduation. In addition, DYRS has never successfully coordinated regular, consistent family visitation for children thousands of miles from home. And lastly, reentry planning is nightmarish as DYRS staff and staff at the contracted facilities have zero process for coordination and frequently fail miserably. As just one example, I once had a client who was nearly discharged onto the streets of Cincinnati until I personally stepped in to let DYRS know that his family wasn't there to pick him up. They'd since moved to Texas, but no one at DYRS knew that. Although that case was from many years ago, it highlights just how wrong things can go without diligent coordination. Outsourcing the care of our children to for-profit entities thousands of miles away simply is not a solution to our public safety woes. We need to do the hard work of wrapping around them with all the love, care, accountability, and support they deserve. I am confident that we have enough expertise, compassion, and commitment to do so right here in the District.

But that brings me to the second area where I urge Council oversight. As I've testified previously, DC –and DYRS in particular- urgently need to get serious about providing robust, well-coordinated, quality community-based services. The public safety crisis we're currently in is due in large part to the persistent failure to provide the financial supports and therapeutic services children and families need to thrive. We cannot incarcerate our way out of this – sending kids thousands of miles away from their friends and loved ones is only going to make them angry, resentful, and skeptical of DYRS care coordinators and service providers when they come home.

In 2009, after years of tough work, DC successfully closed the nightmarish Oak Hill Youth Center and opened New Beginnings. We transitioned from a 180-bed facility to a 60-bed facility. We did that by expanding and enhancing community-based supports. DC established one of the most robust block grant programs ever to exist in my two decades at Open City Advocates. The initiative was called DC Youth Link - a public/private partnership between the DC Trust, two lead entities, and dozens of community-based organizations and service providers.

Sadly, after lauding its praises and touting DC as one of the best juvenile justice systems in the country, the visionary leadership of DYRS moved on. And the DC YouthLink initiative struggled, fizzled, and puttered out. I cannot underscore enough the importance of strong, visionary leadership, both by the Director of DYRS and the DC Council. We need those holding the purse strings and the power to galvanize our community and pull us together to wrap around our children…..not send them thousands of miles away. Out of sight might be out of mind, but only temporarily. Our youth deserve to be top of mind, centered in our hearts, and loved and supported right here in their city. Our city.

Thank you for this opportunity to testify. I welcome any questions.

## Testimony of Joshua A. Miller, PhD, Research & Advocacy Director

Good morning, Chairman White and members of the committee. My name is Joshua Miller. I am a resident of Ward 4 and Open City Advocates' Research & Advocacy Director. I am here today to testify regarding provisions of the Secure DC Omnibus bill that directly implicate DYRS and ought properly to be considered by this committee.

Section 4 of the proposed legislation addresses confidentiality protections for young people committed to the Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services. These provisions give the Office of the Attorney General broad access to information not previously shared with prosecutors or defense attorneys, to be broadcast further to victims and witnesses involved in delinquency cases at the sole discretion of the Attorney General.

### 1. This Section Has Not Received a Public Hearing

OCA has four main substantive objections, but it's worth noting that while this has been touted as a small change and common-sense clarification, it is in fact a major change with potentially far-reaching implications that has been included in the Omnibus without previously being subject to public hearing. So we would ask as a procedural matter that the Councilmembers on this committee move to remove this provision from the Secure DC Omnibus. If necessary, it can be included with other OAG requests regarding confidentiality and data-sharing in another bill to be publicly deliberated.

In general, we believe that the creation of a separate stream of personal treatment and progress data directly to the District's youth prosecutors should give Council pause. You should ask:

1. How will they use this information in future cases involving committed youth?
2. How will the notification requirement influence decision-making by DYRS?
3. Will the clinical evaluations of therapeutic and support staff be affected by the prospect of OAG review or second-guessing?

At the very least, DYRS and OAG should be offering public testimony on these questions.

### 2. The Confidentiality Provisions Violate the Principle of Youth Rehabilitation

Provisions of section 4 require DYRS to provide OAG with notification any time a child returns to the community, for any length of time, including home passes and potentially non-secure group homes. Earlier versions of the legislation included all level and placement information. OAG is explicitly seeking this information for its own purposes as well as to share (at its sole discretion) with victims and witnesses in delinquency cases. Our main concern is that providing this detailed information to OAG and community members violates the principle that young people caught up in the delinquency system should be provided services and treatments and not punished or stigmatized–the goal is rehabilitation, successful reentry and the creation of productive citizens.¹

It is essential to the ethos and structure of the juvenile justice system to allow young people to learn from their mistakes in order to grow into responsible adults without the stigma associated with a criminal history--that is why we focus on rehabilitation and therapeutic treatments over more punitive measures. DYRS is charged with ensuring the rehabilitation of committed youth in its care, and a big part of the juvenile justice system is ensuring a safe transition back to the community. The decision to lower a youth's level of security is not taken lightly and usually requires a youth to undergo a long treatment process. By the time a young person makes it back to the community, DYRS has determined that they are ready to move forward with their life.

This provision runs counter to the spirit of rehabilitation by marking young people and suggesting that, once released, they always need to be watched because they will most likely continue to offend in the future. That's not true!²

### 3. The New Notification Requirement is Unnecessary

The Attorney General and his designee already have access to release information at their request. Indeed, the Office of the Attorney General represents DYRS in court proceedings, including when OCA are forced to litigate to protect children's rights. However, by creating a mandatory notification, we are spreading children's confidential treatment information across multiple government agencies, and onward to the community, building a record that will breach confidentiality in many unexpected ways. And by freeing the OAG to report these records out to the public, the provision would open the door to a larger audience. If educational or medical records had this kind of carve out for their confidentiality, then we would stop thinking of those privacy rights as sacrosanct. In a sense the record of a child's rehabilitative journey just is the record of their education and therapeutic progress. This is more than a slippery slope: this a slip-and-slide towards further criminalizing committed young people.

### 4. The New Notification Requirements Could be Subject to Future Abuse

In some cases a future OAG might use notification provisions to delay a child's release or to demand changes in placement. Other states use victim notification rules to exercise extra-judicial and unilateral control over incarcerated adults. While nothing in the provisions of Section 4 creates this explicit power, we have observed that notification rules can create new delays that could prevent timely release, or might have a chilling effect on DYRS staff who come to believe that OAG would look unfavorably on a weekend pass or change in risk assessment. This inappropriately diverts DYRS's attention from the rehabilitation of the child and puts it on public perceptions. This is likely to have compounding effects in the current climate of fear mongering about young "superpredators."

If Council intends to provide the Office of the Attorney General with increased oversight over the actions of DYRS this should be done explicitly and with greater deliberation than a drop-in provision in an Omnibus bill. We believe that would be better vested in a more neutral ombudsperson, not the District's youth prosecutors.

### 5. The New Notification Requirement Ignores Children's Safety Interests

These notification provisions will provide OAG with information that is not even given to a child's post-commitment attorney under current practice. Juvenile delinquency cases are frequently complex, and the victims or witnesses in one case can be perpetrators or abusers in another case. Unfortunately, we have seen clients targeted by some in their community upon release, and the broadcast of a child's location and domicile in these cases should not be a unilateral decision by prosecutors.

If there will be such rules, defense and post-commitment attorneys must be included in the decision-making. In particular, the child and their attorneys must be provided with a list of all victims and witnesses who are receiving notifications, and be given an opportunity to challenge the inclusion of anyone known to the child or their attorney to represent a potential risk to the child. We believe that this should likely be done in a judicial environment by supplying a pre-approved list of people seeking notification to children and their legal representatives, with the potential to contest the inclusion of names with cause.

### 6. In Summary

We respectfully request that you:

1\. Amend the Secure DC Omnibus to remove all of Section 4.

If that's not possible, then:

2a. Amend Section 4 to remove subsection e.1.A on line 482 and 483: "(A) A committed youth is released to the community from a hardware or staff secure facility, regardless of the length of release; or"

If that's not possible, then:

3\. Amend Section 4 to remove "regardless of the length of release" from subsection e.1.A on line 482 and 483: "(A) A a committed youth is released to the community from a hardware or staff secure facility, regardless of the length of release; or"

4\. Amend Section 4 to remove eyewitnesses and their attorneys from the list in f.1 and f.2 of those informed of releases. (lines 494, 495, 496, and 497)

If that's not possible, then:

5\. Amend Section 4 to change subsection (f)(1) and (f)(2) beginning on line 490 to add pre-registration and approval of all those receiving notifications, to read:

> "(f) Notwithstanding subsection (a)(5) of this section, unless the release of the information is otherwise prohibited by law or the information relates to medical, dental, or mental health appointments, the Attorney General, at the Attorney General's discretion, may disclose information received from the Department pursuant to subsection (e) of this section to:
>
> "(1) An approved list of victims, eyewitnesses, or any duly authorized attorney of any victim or witness whose names have been shared with defense and post-commitment counsel and approved by a DC Superior Court judge;
>
> "(2) An approved list of immediate family members or custodians of any victim or eyewitness, if the victim or eyewitness is a child or if the victim is deceased or incapacitated, or any duly authorized attorney of such immediate family member or custodian whose names have been shared with defense and post-commitment counsel and approved by a Superior Court judge; or
>
> "(3) The parent or guardian of the committed youth.".

## Testimony of Raymond Ngu, Supervising Attorney

Good morning, Chairman White and members of the Committee on Recreation, Libraries, and Youth Affairs. My name is Raymond Ngu and I am a resident of Ward 2 and the Supervising Attorney at Open City Advocates. We provide direct legal services to young people committed to the Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services, as well as re-entry assistance to those returning to the community. In my role, I routinely work with clients at the two DYRS-operated facilities, the Youth Services Center (YSC) and New Beginnings, as well as DYRS youth who are placed in the local committed group home and out-of-state facilities, including in Maryland and Pennsylvania. As an organization, we regularly engage with DYRS staff and leadership, including the Office of the General Counsel, to troubleshoot issues and we appreciate the agency's willingness to work with us to best serve the needs of our clients.

Today I would like to focus on the considerable length of time that committed youth spend awaiting placement and our recommendation for DYRS to find more community-based placements to address this issue. Once a youth is committed to DYRS, the agency should work quickly to identify a placement for that youth to receive clinically appropriate services; these placements can include community-based options that keep youth close to their families, such as group homes and foster homes, but DYRS also relies heavily on out-of-state residential and psychiatric facilities.

In recent months, the awaiting placement process has been plagued with issues, which has resulted in committed youth waiting egregious amounts of time, sometimes up to 8 months, for an appropriate placement. To put that in perspective: the average length of stay at New Beginnings is approximately 9-12 months; this means that there are some youth who are simply awaiting placement for nearly the entire length of the treatment program at New Beginnings. This is unacceptable. Awaiting placement for such a long time not only exacts a large emotional and physical toll on committed youth and their families, but during this time, they are not receiving the services that DYRS itself has said that they need.

We have heard much over the last several months about capacity issues at YSC and New Beginnings, in no small part due to the large number of youth awaiting placement at these facilities. In addressing this issue, DYRS should not simply opt to expand capacity at YSC or create more contracts with out-of-state facilities across the country, which are harmful for a number of reasons. A family with a young person committed to DYRS should not have to board a plane in order to see their child, but that is the reality today. And because these facilities are often so far away, it is impossible for DYRS to provide proper oversight–these privately-operated and for-profit facilities lack accountability and can create extremely harmful conditions for youth, including overmedication and over-reliance on psychotropic drugs, staff abuse of young people, improper use of physical and chemical restraints, and a lack of credit transfers for education purposes, among others.¹

Instead, we strongly urge DYRS to prioritize placement options that allow our young people to stay with their families and communities. This would allow DYRS to address its capacity issues, significantly decrease its awaiting placement population, and best serve the needs of our young people and communities by keeping families together rather than tearing them apart.

Community-based placements serve as critical alternatives to incarceration. The benefit of these placements is that youth can receive regular access to services, including mental health support, that they can continue to access even beyond their commitment to DYRS. This continuum of care allows them the opportunity to learn how to integrate services into their everyday schedules, which may also include school, work, and family time.

As it stands, however, there is currently only one local DC group home for youth committed to DYRS. If that group home is at capacity, which it often is, then a young person may have to wait at a secure facility until a spot opens up even if they have been deemed clinically ready to be discharged. This is not only unfair to the individual young person, it goes against DYRS's rehabilitative mission to place youth in the least restrictive setting that is appropriate for their individual case. To put it plainly, in these instances, young people are being held in secure facilities not because they have been deemed a public safety risk; in fact, they have been deemed ready to return to the community, but because DYRS does not have a community-based placement for them, they remain behind bars. This is a fundamental injustice that violates the spirit of the juvenile justice system.

All of these issues are heightened for committed girls because there is currently no local DC group home for committed girls at all. This, coupled with the fact that there is no girls unit at New Beginnings, means that committed girls are held at YSC until they are placed at an out-of-state facility, if they are accepted to any of those at all. What this looks like in practice is that girls are being held at YSC indefinitely, which has led to overcrowding and reports that some girls have to sleep in classrooms. YSC is designed to be a short-term pre-trial detention facility; it is on a small site, it has little outdoor space or access to fresh air, and limited programming. However, in part due to the lack of community-based options, DYRS is forced to rely on out-of-state facilities to place committed girls; and when those places choose not to accept a particular youth, the child remains at YSC despite the fact that it is "not a treatment facility," to quote DYRS Director Abed.² If more community-based options were available, DYRS could choose to place girls in the community rather than letting them languish at YSC. But they currently do not have that option.

We understand that DYRS has plans to open up new shelter homes, which will have the capacity to convert to group homes depending on need. We applaud those efforts. We urge DC Council to inquire about the status of these contracts and continue to provide intensive oversight on this particular issue. We hope that the agency will consider the concerns raised in our testimony in deciding whether those spaces would be best used as shelter homes or as spaces that could benefit committed youth.

Thank you for the opportunity to testify today. I am available for questions.
