**Recidivism Reduction, Oversight, and Accountability for DYRS Act of 2024 (ROAD Act)**  
**Committee of the Whole, Council of the District of Columbia**  
**Chairman Phil Mendelson**

**Wednesday, September 25, 2024**

## Testimony of Whitney Louchheim, Co-Founder & Chief Operating Officer

### Oral Testimony

Good afternoon, Chairman Mendelson, Councilmember Parker, and members of the Committee of the Whole. My name is Whitney Louchheim, I am a resident of Ward 6, and for the past 19 years have served as the Co-Founder and Chief Operating Officer of Open City Advocates, which provides legal representation and holistic advocacy to youth who have been committed to the DC Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services or DYRS.

I have been working with children committed to DYRS for more than 20 years, and I have observed both heartbreaking trauma and the joy that young people experience when they can thrive and grow in their own communities.

Despite the flowers out front, the inside of the Youth Services Center is comprised of cinder block walls, cold cells, and plastic mattresses. YSC is the sound of sally port doors slamming and children banging their fists on their small, scratched windows….asking to be seen. We have clients who have spent months and months at YSC, receiving few services and decompensating while they languished. We’re disappointed that we’ve sometimes had to bring DYRS to court to challenge this kind of warehousing of our clients. Youth come out of secure detention a year or two older and therefore with more responsibilities and expectations, but without the experience to be prepared for those resposibilities. A teenager who’s being told what to do at all times has no opportunity to be a normal adolescent, practice independence, develop autonomy and mastery, or choose their peers. Incarceration stunts the development of our young people at a crucial moment in their growth–one that can’t fully be recaptured later. DYRS and all of our District government needs to recommit itself to treating kids as kids and using the least restrictive environment consistent with public safety. But that’s not happening right now.

In our work, we regularly witness the premature death of childhood, as one of my colleagues once put it. There are currently dozens of young people at YSC awaiting placement for months at a time. Young people call this “dead time:” DYRS staff shortages routinely mean they have limited access to school or exercise, they do not receive in-depth mental health services, and they receive no “time served” credit towards New Beginnings or any other placement. On weekends there often aren’t enough staff for them to leave their cells and units for extended periods of time, and they quickly decompensate from being separated from their families and communities. Meanwhile, their hope is depleted and the spark of joy in their eyes fades.

On my reading, the ROAD Act is a minor change to the law that nonetheless would clarify DYRS’s obligations to the children under their care for all involved. It provides mechanisms for accountability with an emphasis on DYRS doing what its own policies say should be happening but has not been occuring, especially when it comes to individual development planning, reentry planning, and kids awaiting placement. This legislation is necessary because we as a city are failing to uphold our own values when it comes to the confinement for our city’s children.

DYRS has always been required to complete an individualized treatment plan, but in all the time we have been practicing, we have never seen one. An individualized treatment plan should outline a child’s special educational, psychological, disability, social, and economic needs and background, along with a specific plan for DYRS to tailor its services and placements to the child’s individual needs. While we sometimes receive a completed needs assessment through our routine records requests, I can’t remember a single instance in which we received an individualized treatment plan.

The ROAD Act addresses this “individualized rehabilitation plan,” and requires it be completed, reviewed, and amended regularly with an eye to satisfying the child’s needs and goals. This should be based on a validated needs assessment and should outline the services that will best address their history and needs in a holistic way. Given the criminogenic nature of detention itself, I hope this will enhance DYRS’s commitment to less restrictive settings. Since our youth are drawn into delinquency through the same systematic deprivations that make all human beings act inappropriately, a validated individualized rehabilitation plan should seek to address those root causes and remove the barriers to youth’s success.

The ROAD Act would not make it harder for DYRS to do its job. To the contrary, when DYRS employs a team approach and is transparent and collaborative to meet the needs of a child, it makes DYRS’s job easier. In my experience, DYRS hews more closely to its own rehabilitative policies when it collaborates with family, service providers, attorneys, and the youth themselves. DYRS has not been clearing the bar it has set for itself. The ROAD Act gives clarity to DYRS’s standards, helps DYRS to be more transparent, and gives more opportunities for other team members such as parents, attorneys, the court, and DC Council to collaborate and provide oversight. This will only result in better outcomes for our youth.

Thank you for the opportunity to testify today. I am available for questions.

## Testimony of Raymond Ngu, Legal Director

### Oral Testimony

Good afternoon, Chairman Mendelson and members of the Committee of the Whole. My name is Raymond Ngu and I am a resident of Ward 2 and the Legal Director of Open City Advocates. We provide direct legal services to young people committed to the Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services, as well as re-entry assistance to those returning to the community. In my role, I routinely work with clients at the two DYRS-operated facilities, the Youth Services Center (YSC) and New Beginnings, as well as DYRS youth who are placed in local committed group homes and out-of-state facilities, including in Maryland, Pennsylvania, and Florida. As an organization, we regularly engage with DYRS staff and leadership, including the Office of the General Counsel, to troubleshoot issues and we hope that the agency will continue to work with us to best serve the needs of our clients.

We applaud Attorney General Schwalb and his office for introducing the ROAD Act as a way for the District to better meet the needs of youth committed to DYRS. On the whole, the ROAD Act provides a framework that is targeted to efficiently and effectively ensure that young people committed to DYRS are placed in the appropriate placements and receive the appropriate services.

In recent months, the awaiting placement process has been plagued with issues, which has resulted in committed youth waiting egregious amounts of time, sometimes up to 8 months, for an appropriate placement. To put that in perspective: the average length of stay at New Beginnings is approximately 9-12 months (although for some youth it is much longer); this means that there are some youth who are simply awaiting placement for nearly the entire length of the treatment program at New Beginnings. Awaiting placement for such a long time not only exacts a large emotional and physical toll on committed youth and their families, but during this time, they are not receiving the services that DYRS itself has said that they need.

The ROAD Act would ensure that, upon commitment, young people are not confined to an inappropriate secure placement, such as YSC, for longer than 30 days while awaiting placement. We know that YSC, which is where most committed youth await placement, was designed to be a short-term detention facility and is not equipped to provide meaningful services to youth, which DYRS itself has acknowledged. While the agency has said they are making efforts to reduce the length of time that young people are awaiting placement, the ROAD Act would codify that awaiting placement at YSC for longer than 30 days is presumptively inappropriate unless there is good cause shown. This would go a long way to ensuring that committed youth are transferred to appropriate placements quickly. In implementing this provision, we would encourage DYRS to consider its several community placements as options for committed youth to await placement.

The ROAD Act would also require that DYRS engage in meaningful discharge and re-entry planning for youth as soon as they are admitted to a placement that is outside the family home, including secure and out-of-state facilities. Discharge planning is important to ensure that services and programming are available to youth immediately upon their return to the community. This includes school enrollment, mental health services, and recreational programming.

As it currently stands, we have noticed that discharge planning does not occur until just before the youth is scheduled to discharge from a facility; there is also a lack of transparency regarding the discharge process and timeline. This often results in delayed discharges from out-of-home facilities, even when a young person has successfully completed their treatment program, and leads to a gap in services upon discharge. We have noticed that this is especially an issue when it comes to school enrollment, with some clients not getting properly enrolled in community schools for up to several weeks after they have returned. While we understand that some delays are unforeseeable, we believe that it is imperative for the agency to engage in robust discharge planning well in advance of a youth’s anticipated return to the community. This is the only way to ensure that crucial services, including access to education, are maintained as a youth transitions from one placement to the next.

The ROAD Act would require DYRS to start engaging in discharge planning immediately upon a youth’s admittance to any out-of-home facility, including residential facilities, group homes, and therapeutic foster homes. This is best practice for re-entry planning because it looks at a youth’s treatment plan holistically instead of focusing narrowly only on their current placement; in other words, it allows, and in fact requires, the agency to look further ahead into the youth’s case and provide a continuum of care that is consistent with DYRS’ overall mission. We strongly encourage the agency to include the youth, their families, and their attorneys in this discharge planning process so that a young person’s entire team can be part of their successful transition back to the community.

Overall, the ROAD Act would provide clear guidelines to the agency, committed youth, their families, and their advocates about the process and timeline for placements and discharge. This increased transparency and planning will not only give young people a more comprehensive understanding of the agency’s treatment plan for them, and in turn lead to more buy-in, it will also ensure that youth do not experience gaps in services and programming as they transition between placements.

Thank you and I am available for questions.

## Joint Written Testimony

*Testimony of Co-Founder & Chief Operating Officer Whitney Louchheim, Esq.; Legal Director Raymond Ngu, Esq.; Research & Advocacy Director Joshua Miller, PhD*

### Who We Are

Open City Advocates (OCA) is a small organization with a big impact. We model an innovative solution in juvenile defense in Washington, DC, and nationwide. Founded to address the racial inequities and unjust incarceration practices in DC’s juvenile system, OCA provides youth-centered legal defense and holistic advocacy for young people after sentencing in the juvenile system.

### Who We Serve

All of our clients have been committed to the care and custody of DYRS by DC’s Family Court. “Commitment” to DYRS means that a young person is a ward of the city, oftentimes until age 21, and that their placement may change several times with little or no court oversight–including placement at home or in a carceral facility.

There are extreme racial disparities in the youth delinquency system nationwide, with Black and Latino/a children being committed and incarcerated at 4.7 times the rate compared to their white peers.¹ It is even more extreme in the District of Columbia, where 100% of all committed children are children of color, and 93% are Black, while Black youth comprise only 51% of the DC youth population.²

OCA’s clients have had extensive contact with the delinquency system; most have also been in DC’s child welfare system, and all are at high risk for entering the DC Jail or being harmed or killed by street violence. The majority of our clients hail from DC’s Wards 7 and 8, where 35% of children live in poverty.³ Over the last two years, 46% of violent crimes committed with guns in the city occurred in these two wards.⁴ In our 19-year history, we have lost six clients to gun violence and one to a drug overdose. OCA works to help our clients envision and actualize a healthy adulthood instead of accepting death or incarceration by age 25.

### 1. The Commitment Model

The District’s unique approach to juvenile commitment requires significant unpacking, using unfamiliar terminology and evincing a set of values that prioritizes youth well-being, family reunification, and holistic and rehabilitative treatment– that DYRS has sometimes failed to uphold. In particular, DYRS is committed by law to achieve its preventive and rehabilitative goals in “the least restrictive setting necessary, with a preference at all times for the preservation of the family and the integration of parental, guardian, and custodial accountability and participation in treatment and counseling programs.”⁵ It’s hard to overemphasize how often this “least restrictive setting” requirement is violated, or parents are unduly burdened in their efforts to maintain contact with their children or their parental rights.

Though this is not always the case in other states, in DC the neglect and delinquency systems are divided between two different agencies. There is frequent crossover, with some youth actively dual-jacketed with cases in both systems, and more youth with inactive cases in one system but active ones in the other. In a more fundamental sense, juvenile justice in DC is founded on the notion that delinquency is always the outgrowth of caregiver and systemic neglect–for better and for worse: this is why young people are not “found guilty” but “adjudicated involved” and they are not sentenced to a term of incarceration or other punishment, but put on probation or “committed.”

Young people who are adjudicated involved in delinquent acts can be put on probation under the supervision of the Court Social Services Division (CSSD), which is a federally-funded branch of the DC Superior Court, or committed to the “supervision, custody, and care” of DYRS. The agency effectively takes over many parental rights and responsibilities (though parents of committed children do not cede crucial medical or education rights over their children).

The decision whether to place children on probation or commitment is made by judges after adjudication and upon the review of a precommitment report/recommendation that is supposed to be prepared by DYRS Care Coordinators. Most children receive probation, or are diverted even earlier, and indeed, as far as we are aware, this has happened for all white children since we began working in the system more than two decades ago. The court is expected to recommend specific services, treatment modalities, and supervision requirements, but cannot specify a facility. CSSD provides some initial information for this review, often including health insurance status and psychological reports that DYRS relies on in its own assessments. However, without oversight of CSSD, the District is not able to guarantee that the assessments and other information provided by them is always complete and accurate. Thus DYRS can and should perform its own evaluations.

If children are committed to DYRS, judges have fewer opportunities for oversight from that point forward. The child’s placement and services are almost entirely within the purview of DYRS. The agency, and not the judge, will then decide whether to lock the child up in secure detention facilities, whether to put them in a group home or send them out of state, and also whether and when they’re ready to be sent home. It is usually a combination of all the above over the course of years and there is limited access to the courts after the point of commitment. One thing that the ROAD Act does well is provide more opportunities for court oversight.

### 2. Individualized Rehabilitation Plans and Discharge/Reentry Planning

The most important parts of this bill place timelines on DYRS’s completion of individualized rehabilitation plans and require them to be informed by a validated needs assessment that aims for successful reentry. This follows the best practices of the many other states, but it will require an improvement from current practice.

If a child’s commitment requires them to complete a program at New Beginnings, they can expect to move through seven levels of programming. DYRS usually reports this as six levels of positive youth development, in part because it does not have specific programming for the final level, but DYRS has added a significant extra step because of its own failure to begin discharge planning in a timely fashion. Historically, discharge from New Beginnings was supposed to happen within a week or two of reaching this sixth level, but we have observed that as the delays mount, kids become frustrated. DYRS thus added a seventh step: petitioning for release, known as “home petition.” This has grown from a two-week process into an extra forty-four days, at least: DYRS now requires that a child remain at Level 6 for thirty days, successfully petition for the next level which is called “home petition,” and then DYRS will start the process of scheduling a discharge meeting after two weeks of maintaining good behavior. (And at that meeting they are likely to learn of obstacles to discharge that may require additional days and weeks of incarceration.) In practice, this means that a process that should be as short as six months in the best case scenario now requires between nine and eighteen months, and almost always longer than a year.

Much of this extra time is generated by the bureaucratic delays caused by waiting so long to begin discharge planning. School enrollment, identifying foster homes or ensuring adequate space and safety in the family home, lining up community-based providers for referrals, and completing all the paperwork takes time. Moreover, as children spend longer and longer feeling that they have completed all the expectations set for them without the promised reward of freedom, it gets harder for some of them to maintain positive behavior, which can lead to further delays that are then blamed on them: indeed, they see this temporizing as a violation of the implicit contract the program promises.

In our view, Level 4, dubbed “Independence,” is the right time to start reentry and discharge planning, and according to DYRS’s most recent Care Coordination Handbook from 2017, this is precisely when discharge planning is supposed to begin. Sadly, DYRS no longer follows this discharge planning timeline. Kids most often stall at the start of the program, stuck at level 1 or 2. Thus a child who has completed level 4 is likely to complete the rest of the program on schedule, is two or three months away from completing the program, and is ready to begin concrete planning for a return to the community.

The cost of making last-minute decisions with uncertain timelines is that neither schools nor community placements can keep spots open indefinitely.

The average length of stay at New Beginnings is approximately 9-12 months; this means that there are some youth who are simply awaiting placement for nearly the entire length of the treatment program at New Beginnings. Awaiting placement for such a long time not only exacts a large emotional and physical toll on committed youth and their families, but during this time, they are not receiving the services that both the court and DYRS itself has said that they need.

### 4. Community-Based Placements

The detention of children is fundamentally traumatizing.⁶ It fills our children with a hurt and rage that they will carry beyond those walls, often manifesting in ways that do great harm. As DC’s Criminal Justice Coordinating Council has stated repeatedly, “While the juvenile justice system is intended to rehabilitate children, involvement in the system, particularly secure detention, is well-established to have lasting negative effects on youth such as increased risk of adult incarceration, decreased likelihood of high school graduation and success in the labor market, and worsening of mental health disorders.”⁷

Research shows that incarceration greatly disrupts educational progress and can reduce the likelihood of high school graduation by 26%. And after release, it is typically our staff attorneys fighting to get our clients re-enrolled in school – not the judges, probation officers, or case workers who claimed that a child needed to be locked up in order to get an education.

As discussed above, the best evidence suggests that youth who spend more than six months in detention stop receiving any desistance benefits, and that even shorter periods of time can be traumatic. When we record our clients’ adverse childhood experiences, incarceration is always among them. And we see the chronic stress and ongoing PTSD that these periods of detention can cause firsthand. (Both family separation from caregivers and the incarceration of a family member can be traumatic–how much more traumatic is one’s own incarceration?)

There is a desperate need for more community-based placements and community-based services for committed youth. We strongly support family placements with robust wraparound services as the most effective and most therapeutic placements for youth. Community-based placements, such as foster care and independent living programs, can also play a much larger role in closing the gap between “hardware secure” facilities that are ultimately prisons no matter how much we euphemize them. They can serve an important function by allowing youth the opportunity to transition into the community from a secure setting, while also providing close support and programming options. They also serve as critical alternatives to incarceration. According to the Sentencing Project, the District of Columbia already has the third highest detention rate of young people per capita among states in America.⁸ We have heard much over the last several years about capacity issues at YSC and New Beginnings–we strongly urge DYRS to prioritize placement options that allow our young people to stay with their families and communities, rather than building new beds at YSC or forming new contracts with residential facilities across the country.

As it stands, however, there are limited group home beds for committed youth. This means that if that group home is at capacity, which it often is, then a young person may have to wait at a secure facility until a spot opens up even if they have been deemed clinically ready to be discharged. This is not only unfair to the individual young person, it goes against DYRS’s rehabilitative mission to place youth in the least restrictive setting that is appropriate for their individual case. To put it plainly, in these instances, young people are being held in secure facilities not because they have been deemed a public safety risk; in fact, they have been deemed ready to return to the community, but because DYRS does not have a community-based placement for them, they remain securely detained. This is a fundamental injustice that violates the spirit of the juvenile justice system.

All of these issues are heightened for committed girls because there is currently no local DC group home for committed girls at all. Until the opening of the girls unit at New Beginnings, which is DYRS’s primary long-term facility, this meant that committed girls are held at YSC until they are placed at an out-of-state facility, if they are accepted to any of those at all. What this looks like in practice is that girls were being held at YSC for unacceptably long periods of time, which led to overcrowding and reports that some girls have to sleep in classrooms. YSC is designed to be a short-term pre-trial detention facility; it is on a small site, and it has little outdoor space and limited programming. However, in part due to the lack of community-based options, DYRS is forced to rely on out-of-state facilities to place committed girls; and when those places choose not to accept a particular youth, they remain at YSC indefinitely despite the fact that YSC is “not a treatment facility,” to quote DYRS Director Abed. If more community-based options were available, DYRS could choose to place girls there rather than letting them languish at YSC. But they currently do not have that option.

We believe that community-based placements are always more beneficial and therapeutic for young people compared to detention, and we would also like to ensure that they operate as intended. The benefit of these placements is that youth can receive regular access to services, including mental health support, that they can continue to access even beyond their commitment to DYRS. This continuum of care allows them the opportunity to learn how to integrate services into their everyday schedules, which may also include school, work, and family time.

However, in order to keep youth engaged and invested in their own success, robust programming and services need to be made available. In our experience, local group homes for committed youth typically do not provide the level of programming that would be consistent with DYRS’s mission. While youth in a group home can be taken to DYRS facilities, such as the Achievement Centers, for programming, it is our understanding that there are little to no activities that are being programmed by the group homes themselves. This means that during summers, school breaks, weekends, and evenings, youth in group homes are left with very little to do if they are not being brought to a DYRS facility. This creates a vacuum that leads to boredom and frustration, and is often a contributing factor leading to abscondences.

We frequently hear from these placements that the delays in transfers and communications go on so long (weeks and months at a time) that they assume DYRS has lost interest in the referral and give the spot to someone else. Shockingly, DYRS staff then report these failures as rejections of our clients–and though we can’t be certain, it seems that they may also report these as rejections in aggregate in oversight hearings to the DC Council and in planning meetings on the challenge of children awaiting placement.

We continue to hope that DYRS will work with their group home operators to ensure that those charged with housing and supervising our young people have the direction, resources, and training they need to provide engaging and supportive programming.

### 5. Therapeutic Foster Care

Foster care is a common solution in neglect and abuse cases, and it should be utilized more often in the delinquency system as well. For a variety of reasons, a home placement with family can sometimes be unworkable or impossible for children committed to the delinquency system. In such cases, the foster care system is almost always preferable to more institutional settings. However, some children with a high level of assessed need may find that they need more support than a traditional foster care family can provide. In these cases, there is an alternative: therapeutic foster care.⁹

There are two evidence-based models for Therapeutic or Treatment Foster Care (TFC): Treatment Foster Care Oregon¹⁰ (which used to be called Multidimensional Treatment Foster Care, MTFC) and Together Facing the Challenge (TFTC)¹¹. Correctly implemented, TFC can be used to serve high-needs children in the community, as an alternative to hardware-secure facilities. Doing so requires that a jurisdiction build infrastructure to support the service–recruitment, retention, training and coaching, evaluation, and respite care–because TFC families take on tremendous challenges and will need a break. It also means paying the TFC family well–the usual expectation is that the family takes only one foster child and the foster parent spends significant time engaged in therapy, supervision, with the school, and bio families/kin if possible/appropriate.

We applaud DYRS for offering foster care as a community-based placement option, but TFC thus requires a serious, long-term commitment to using evidence-based models and to maintaining fidelity with those models. Unfortunately, DYRS’s contracts with Maryland TFC providers are best understood as  “Therapeutic Foster Care” in name only–they are foster care placements of varying quality that are a welcome supplement to other placements, but cannot act as a true substitute for intensive treatment institutions for high needs kinds (as they do in other states) because the MD providers do not meet the minimum standards for model fidelity to the provision of supports.

Better implementation of the evidence-based practices of TFC in the District and our local region is thus an essential component in reducing our overreliance on incarceration. We would urge DYRS to 1) contract for more foster care beds, 2) revise practices and procedures to prefer TFC contracts whenever possible over secure detention facilities, and 3) lean into the therapeutic model to enhance the quality of their foster care options, exerting more exacting contractual requirements on their providers. Like many other elements of the system, demanding model fidelity can be hard to do when the providers are beyond their capacity because of overcrowding issues elsewhere in the system but this TFC could potentially lighten the load of staff at DYRS while reducing the number of high needs children exposed to the trauma of longterm incarceration.

We also have good reason to believe that independent living providers can work for those young people who don’t have access to community placements with caretakers in a parent or kin home. DYRS sometimes develops contracts with independent living programs, such as Covenant House, to provide a structured placement option for older youth who are ready to begin learning the skills of adulthood. This is a key component of a complete continuum of community-based placements, particularly for youth who do not have the option of a family home and who have aged out of other potential placements such as foster care. We would also encourage DYRS to expand its independent living options.

### 6. Extending the Medicaid Continuum of Care

While incarcerated youth have generally been excluded from Medicaid eligibility, a recent change to the federal appropriation law **requires** state Medicaid and CHIP programs to provide both adults and young people in carceral and juvenile justice agencies certain screenings, diagnostic services, case management, and referrals 30 days before release and 30 days after discharge. It also **allows** states to apply for a waiver to expand those services up to 90 days before and after discharge. We favor the mandated expansion of Medicaid-covered services, especially prevention, screening, and wellness services, and we are enthusiastic about the prospect of Medicaid-funded reentry support by community-based providers creating a continuum of care that begins during incarceration under the 1115 waiver process. At the same time, we have serious concerns about the capacity of our behavioral health system to meet the needs of incarcerated youth, and that DYRS may not be prepared to implement these new services with an attention to access and quality.

The District of Columbia is required to create an internal operational plan before January 1, 2025, that describes how it will meet the following mandates and requirements:¹²

1. Screening and diagnostic services, including “any screenings and diagnostic services which meet reasonable standards of medical and dental practice, as determined by the state, or as otherwise indicated as medically necessary, in accordance with the Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment (EPSDT) requirements, including a behavioral health screening or diagnostic services.”¹³ This includes immunizations.
2. Targeted Case Management, including
   1. “Comprehensive assessment and periodic reassessment of individual needs, to determine the need for any medical, educational, social, or other services;
   2. Development (and periodic revision) of a specific person-centered care plan based on the information collected through the assessment;
   3. Referral and related activities (such as scheduling appointments for the individual) to help the eligible individual obtain needed services, including activities that help link the individual with medical, social, and educational providers or other programs and services that are capable of providing needed services to address identified needs and achieve goals specified in the care plan; and
   4. Monitoring and follow-up activities, including activities and contacts that are necessary to ensure that the care plan is effectively implemented and adequately addresses the needs of the eligible individual and which may be with the individual, family members, service providers, or other entities or individuals and conducted as frequently as necessary, and including at least one annual monitoring.”¹⁴
3. Referrals to community-based providers of behavioral health services “in the geographic area where the eligible juvenile will be residing upon release, whenever possible.”¹⁵
4. A “warm handoff” for any new case manager that includes a meeting between the young person and both case managers and a review of the care plan and steps to ensure continuity.¹⁶

Beyond the requirements, DC has also submitted a request for waiver under Medicaid’s 1115 rule, which would allow an extension of these required services from 90 days before release and 90 days afterwards. This is particularly important since it would allow significant overlap from community-based providers, and absorb some of the costs associated with this enhanced level of care.

However, one crucial element of youth commitment to DYRS is that there is rarely clarity about when, exactly, a period of confinement in a secure facility will end. This indeterminacy is tied to the nature of the behavioral modifications required for advancement and release, and the limited supply of community placements. For committed youth at YSC awaiting a community-based placement, release dates are driven by the availability of placements and other logistics. For youth at New Beginnings, release dates are based on completion of programming, which can vary widely.

These changes present an opportunity for a significant improvement in the operation of DYRS reentry planning. Yet this new mandate requires just such clarity to determine when a young person will begin to qualify for Medicaid–subsidized release services. Giving children and the Medicaid system a clear and credible release date will thus require a review of Team Decision Meeting processes and the implementation of significant case management changes. If this were implemented honestly, it would be severely underutilized since the final steps of release planning are often done only days or even hours before a child is released despite constant pressure from advocates to start agency planning earlier. There is also a risk that only small tweaks to the current system would lead to serious overutilization, with release dates constantly pushed back after 90 days of Medicaid services have been consumed. Thus, in order to effectively implement this waiver, discharge planning will need to begin upon a youth's entry into a facility.  This planning should include an anticipated or expected release date in order to maximize the utilization and benefit of the waiver.

Practically, DYRS must review their discharge planning processes and make the necessary changes to its policies relating to team decision-making meetings and case management. A change in these practices is desperately needed to allow for the waiver to be effective and to provide discharge planning with fidelity for youth.

While the District recently authored Emergency Intensive Care Coordination Regulations providing for High Fidelity Wraparound model services to address the lack of intensive services for youth, the regulations failed to clearly address the eligibility determination process for ICC services; the fidelity of ICC services; and the transparency of program evaluation. Given the historic concerns, policy changes through emergency regulations or the 1115 waivers do not change the ineffective foundation of service delivery on which these new policies are based.

High-Fidelity Wraparound is an evidence-informed, strength-based process “led by a Care Coordinator who is responsible for collaborating with a team-based planning process where representatives of multiple systems come together with a child, youth, [sic], and their family to create a highly individualized plan to address the complex emotional needs of a child or youth.”¹⁷ Yet there is not adequate provision of High-Fidelity Wraparound in the District, despite the fact that High-Fidelity Wraparound services are associated with high rates of success in the community in other jurisdictions, including massive reductions in recidivism, ongoing sustained housing, and major improvements in school attendance. From our experience, providers are already struggling to fill positions and maintain capacity to properly serve youth and adults. Unfortunately, the proposal to use current providers will further strain a system already suffering from staffing deficiencies resulting in fragmented mental health services.

What’s more, there are also significant gaps in the community-based treatment system: for instance, there is currently only one provider in the District certified in the multisystem therapy modality which is particularly useful to system-impacted youth aged 12-17, and is “desperately need” but the one provider cannot meet demand.¹⁸ As well, there are reportedly no programs in the District that take Medicaid and also provide access to monthly injectable buprenorphine, which has become an essential tool for opioid addiction treatment in other states. This means that District residents with opioid use disorders who rely on Medicaid do not have a single community provider that can serve them.

In its performance oversight testimony, the DC Department of Behavioral Health (DBH) usually pegs the capacity for community-based High-Fidelity Wraparound services at 85 young people. In FY18, DBH reported an average time from enrollment to treatment of 33 days for children in the community. For FY23, they reported an average time from referral to intake appointment of 19 days, and an average time from intake to diagnostic assessment of 46 days, for a total of 65 days pre-treatment. They were unable to report time to treatment. This suggests a behavioral health system that is quickly falling behind under various staffing pressures.

Increased Medicaid funding can certainly help alleviate one pressure, but 1115 demonstrations are supposed to be cost neutral, suggesting a reliance on preventative gains and a shift from more expensive and intensive providers in carceral settings to more efficient and effective providers in the community. In order to achieve these gains, we will need to ensure that the capacity of community-based providers, which is currently at a breaking point, is expanded to meet the current and future needs of incarcerated young people seeking release.

### 8. The ROAD Act

The ROAD Act is a minor change to the law that nonetheless would clarify DYRS’s obligations to the children under their care for all involved. It provides mechanisms for accountability with an emphasis on DYRS doing what its own policies say should be happening but has not been occuring, especially when it comes to individual development planning, reentry planning, and kids awaiting placement. This legislation is necessary because we as a city are failing to uphold our own values when it comes to the confinement for our city’s children.

DYRS has always been required to complete an individualized treatment plan, but in all the time we have been practicing, we have never seen one. An individualized treatment plan should outline a child’s special educational, psychological, disability, social, and economic needs and background, along with a specific plan for DYRS to tailor its services and placements to the child’s individual needs. While we sometimes receive a completed needs assessment through our routine records requests, we can’t remember a single instance in which we received an individualized treatment plan.

The ROAD Act addresses this “individualized rehabilitation plan,” and requires it be completed, reviewed, and amended regularly with an eye to satisfying the child’s needs and goals. This should be based on a validated needs assessment and should outline the services that will best address their history and needs in a holistic way. Given the criminogenic nature of detention itself, I hope this will enhance DYRS’s commitment to less restrictive settings. Since our youth are drawn into delinquency through the same systematic deprivations that make all human beings act inappropriately, a validated individualized rehabilitation plan should seek to address those root causes and remove the barriers to youth’s success.

The ROAD Act would not make it harder for DYRS to do its job. To the contrary, when DYRS employs a team approach and is transparent and collaborative to meet the needs of a child, it makes DYRS’s job easier. In our experience, DYRS hews more closely to its own rehabilitative policies when it collaborates with family, service providers, attorneys, and the youth themselves. DYRS has not been clearing the bar it has set for itself. The ROAD Act gives clarity to DYRS’s standards, helps DYRS to be more transparent, and gives more opportunities for other team members such as parents, attorneys, the court, and DC Council to collaborate and provide oversight. This will only result in better outcomes for our youth.
