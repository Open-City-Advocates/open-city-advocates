**DYRS Performance Oversight Hearing**  
**Committee on Youth Affairs, Council of the District of Columbia**  
**Councilmember Zachary Parker, Chair**

**March 6, 2025**

## Testimony of Penelope Spain, Co-Founder & Chief Executive Officer

Good Morning, Councilmembers. My name is Penelope Spain. I am a resident of Ward 4 and the CEO and Co-Founder of Open City Advocates, a nonprofit that has served the District for over two decades by providing legal representation and holistic advocacy to youth committed to the Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services (DYRS). Our work includes significant support for our clients throughout their DYRS commitment and during their reentry from secure facilities. Today, I am focusing my testimony on the importance of communication, collaboration, family and youth engagement, and discharge planning.

### Communication and Collaboration

#### Team Decision Making Meetings

In order to highlight communication and collaboration challenges in a concrete way, I’ll begin by discussing DYRS’s policies and practices regarding Team Decision Making meetings, also known as TDMs. Team Decision Making meetings are meant to be what their name portends—a place where a child’s full team can come together and make decisions regarding how best to meet a child’s underlying needs. These meetings should actively involve the child, their family members, school representatives, and service providers. The DC Court of Appeals has also ruled that a child’s post-disposition attorney, such as those working for my organization, should be there to protect the rights of the child and elevate the child’s expressed interests. Per DYRS policies, TDMs are to be held every 90 days and are coordinated by the child’s DYRS Care Coordinator.

Sadly, in Open City Advocates’ cases, DYRS Care Coordinators are *not* holding TDMs every 90 days. Instead, they seem to be happening once or twice a year and sometimes not at all. In addition, DYRS does not consult with post-disposition attorneys to schedule them at a time that all can attend or provides such late notice that we are often unable to even consult with our clients beforehand. Family members are also not adequately informed about the purpose of the TDM, and they are rarely consulted about the various placement and treatment decisions about their own child.

To truly address a child’s underlying needs and set them on a path towards success, a robust and genuine team approach is critical. Determining services without carefully factoring in the voices of the child (including the child’s attorney), as well as their family, their care providers, and the schools lead to cookie-cutter programs that are not individually-tailored. A perfunctory process conveys to children and their family a lack of concern for their well-being. And children often won’t comply with a program or treatment plan that was created without their buy-in or participation.

To overcome this, TDMs must happen at the right times, include the right people, and avoid superficiality, or the appearance of rote indifference. TDMs should not merely be a quick conversation to inform a child or parent of what has already been decided without them or their active engagement. In terms of timing, we believe that 90 days is generally an appropriate cadence. However, we encourage DYRS, whenever possible, to link these meetings to major milestones or changes in services and placement. At the very least, they should be held immediately before and after commitment, before new placements, regularly during any sort of out-of-state or secure placement, and long before discharge and reentry so that appropriate planning can take place.

#### Communication

Turning to communication in general, DYRS seems to be increasingly unwilling to engage non-DYRS stakeholders in the care and treatment of children. Over the past two decades, Open City Advocates has prided itself on working hand-in-hand with DYRS Care Coordinators to ensure the most effective services and best placements for our clients. While DYRS is ultimately responsible for many of the decisions regarding the treatment of a child, our attorneys are often able to speed along processes or offer alternative solutions that better meet the needs of a child or family. Sadly, such collaboration is now exceedingly rare. Our staff attorneys are frequently unable to collaborate directly with DYRS Care Coordinators as we used to; it is now typical for us to receive radio silence in answer to our emails and phone calls requesting basic information about our clients. When we are able to connect directly with Care Coordinators, they refer our attorneys to DYRS General Counsel. Running each question or clarification through General Counsel is time consuming and often results in conflicting answers. We are grateful for DYRS General Counsel’s collaborative efforts, but they are not positioned to understand the nuances and complexities of each child’s needs: that is the purpose of DYRS care coordination staff. The agency’s staff titles (coordinators of care, representatives of youth development, managers of treatment) are not merely euphemisms for case workers and correctional officers: they’re intended to capture a distinct rehabilitative approach to juvenile justice, which must include a holistic, team approach to be effective.

### Discharge Planning

Lastly, discharge planning still does not occur until just before the youth is scheduled to discharge from a facility, and there is also a lack of transparency regarding the discharge process and timeline. This often results in delayed discharges from out-of-home facilities, even when a young person has successfully completed their treatment program, and leads to a gap in services upon discharge. We have noticed that this is especially an issue when it comes to school reenrollment, with some clients not getting enrolled in community schools for up to several weeks after they have returned. While some delays are understandable and unforeseeable, it is imperative that DYRS engage in robust discharge planning well in advance of a youth’s anticipated return to the community. This is the only way to ensure that crucial services, including access to education, are maintained as a youth transitions from one placement to the next.

In a city where truancy is frequently blamed for delinquency, it’s unconscionable that DYRS itself contributes to school non-attendance. Yet school placements are not a priority for discharge planning and our attorneys are usually doing most of the work to get our clients placed in schools. Of course, our professional responsibilities as attorneys mean that we always step up to meet the needs created by the failure of other systems, but we shouldn’t have to do that when DC government agencies could more easily collaborate internally and together if they prioritized this work.

### Family Engagement

DYRS could also engage families more meaningfully. For example, they seem to be lacking clear protocols for when to notify families about medical, educational, or placement issues regarding their children. Even though parents retain the medical and education rights for their children when their children are committed, DYRS seems to lack a clear process about how and when important parental discussions should happen. For example, we have seen instances when parents were not consulted about medication for their child or were not notified when their child was hospitalized or moved to a new facility. This is something about which Jo Patterson from ParentWatch frequently testifies, and we are grateful for their voice.

Thank you and I am available for questions.

## Testimony of Joshua A. Miller, PhD, Research & Advocacy Director

Good morning. My name is Joshua Miller, and I am the Research & Advocacy Director for Open City Advocates. I have been working with the DC Juvenile Justice Advisory Group over the past year, have served as a liaison to the Youth Leaders in Action subcommittee of the JJAG, and have visited with the YSC Youth Advisory Board for the past six months (including last week). I want to thank you, Councilmember Parker, for making an effort to meet with the children at YSC as well--my understanding is that you met with many of the folks with whom we regularly speak!

Our meetings are always monitored by facility staff, usually led by the Superintendent or Deputy Superintendent. We meet with a hand-selected group, who have tended to be fairly persistent members of the group from month to month. Together they come from many different units and are asked to speak on both the conditions they face and those experienced by others in the same housing unit.

Some concerns have been perennial. The young people often discuss needed programming, and complain about large amounts of downtime. They often request instruction in vocational skills and business. They worry that their time at YSC is "dead time" and doesn't allow them to make progress towards release. They mention how infrequently they get information from care coordinators, often going months without an update. They complain about the quality of the hygiene products, and note that they're frequently inappropriate for African-American hair and skin. (It's like the old joke: the quality is terrible, and such small portions!) They mention the paucity of books (and especially culturally-appropriate books) and complain about the selections.

On that last issue, I'm proud to say that the Youth Leaders in Action have partnered with the DC Public Library to get a grant to provide culturally appropriate graphic novels, which we also delivered last week. Congressman John Lewis's 3-volume graphic novel March was a particular hit, as was Black Panther.

These are significant issues, but also normal problems in carceral facilities. Every so often a long conversation will reveal something surprising or worrisome, and I'd like to focus on these.

### Awaiting Placement

One problem, worth special note, is how often we meet with the same young people month after month. This short term facility with little rehabilitative and therapeutic programming has many young people who end up stuck in limbo for months at a time, and while we don't inquire about their cases sometimes they let slip little details that suggest a shocking lack of coordination.

### Hygiene

Sometimes discussions of hygiene product availability and generic complaints about sharing space with other young people suggest deeper problems. For instance, young men aren't allowed to have the same kinds of head coverings that they'd use in the community and that young women are allowed, for instance--and after months this policy has gone unchanged. I think staff honestly want to see the policy changed, but it seems to have taken longer than it needs. But every so often they'll mention in passing that others on their unit go days and weeks without bathing at all. While these observations are voiced as complaints, when I hear about a young person completely neglecting self-care I worry that this suggests depression, fear, or decompensation. For instance, CCE's preliminary research has suggested that less than half of young people at YSC consistently feel safe, and that may be showing itself in an unwillingness to use the showers.

I think it's important that members of the JJAG YLA continue to meet with young people at YSC and maintain this rapport. I'm grateful to Director Abed for allowing the sessions to continue, and to you, Councilmember Parker, for meeting with the young people directly.
