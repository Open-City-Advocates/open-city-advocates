In October 2025, Open City Advocates noticed a startling claim in sworn D.C. Council testimony: DYRS Director Sam Abed said the agency was seeking to acquire or lease a Maryland detention facility near New Beginnings. The description pointed to the shuttered Thomas J. S. Waxter Children’s Center. Reports from Maryland’s Juvenile Justice Monitoring Unit supplied the essential documentary record, showing years of mold, sewage, pests, structural failures, and other dangerous conditions—and repeated official conclusions that Waxter was unsuitable for secure detention. Abed himself had overseen its closure while serving as Maryland’s juvenile-services secretary.

We synthesized that evidence into a November 6 press statement asking whether DYRS intended to reopen Waxter for D.C. children. We circulated it to roughly twenty journalists, advocates, elected officials, and government staff and presented the findings in D.C. Council testimony. The monitoring unit’s reports allowed us to transform a troubling but vague proposal into a specific, well-supported public challenge.

The campaign created an important bridge between D.C. and Maryland advocates. Bethany Young of DC Justice Lab encouraged a coordinated D.C.–Maryland response and helped identify potential Maryland partners. Alice Wilkerson of Advance Maryland then mobilized Maryland’s youth-justice network and engaged state and Anne Arundel County officials. Alicia Yass of ACLU-D.C. coordinated with ACLU of Maryland Public Policy Director Yanet Amanuel, leading the two affiliates to issue a joint statement opposing Waxter’s reopening. Anne Arundel Council Chair Julie Hummer explained that county leaders had been pursuing community use of the property and were prepared to organize against its return to detention.

The work ultimately exposed a serious discrepancy in DYRS’s account. A December 1 Washington Post investigation by Jenny Gathright and Katie Mettler reported that Abed had contacted Maryland about Waxter in August—and had already been told that the property was unavailable because the state was discussing its transfer to Anne Arundel County for community use. The proposed prison did not advance; instead, the campaign produced cross-jurisdictional opposition, press scrutiny, and a clearer record of what DYRS knew and when it knew it.
