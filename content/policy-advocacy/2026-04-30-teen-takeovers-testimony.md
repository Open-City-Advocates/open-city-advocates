**Before the Committee on Youth Affairs**  
**Council of the District of Columbia**  
**Special Hearing: Youth-Centered Alternatives to Teen Takeovers: Recreation, Opportunity, and Accountability**

**April 30, 2026**

**Written Testimony of Joshua Miller, Ph.D., Research and Advocacy Director**

Chairperson Parker and members of the Committee, thank you for the opportunity to submit written testimony. My name is Joshua Miller. I serve as Research and Advocacy Director at Open City Advocates, a DC legal organization that has provided post-disposition representation to young people committed to the Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services for twenty-one years.

I am grateful to Chairperson Parker for voting against B26-0461 and for using the chair of this Committee to convene a hearing on what the District should be doing instead. Both moves are correct, and both are too rare. Most Council conversations about youth gatherings in commercial corridors run in the other direction, toward broader enforcement authority and less scrutiny of what enforcement actually produces. The framing this Committee has chosen, alternatives rather than escalation, concedes a real point: the District has a youth presence problem because it has a youth infrastructure problem, and we will not enforce our way out of it.

OCA does not represent every young person who shows up at Navy Yard or H Street on a Friday night. The clients I see are the back end of the system. I see the young people whose social life under bad conditions moved, often through one bad evening and one bad encounter, into court involvement, commitment to DYRS, and the collateral consequences that follow. From that vantage point, I offer this Committee four observations to support its continued opposition to B26-0461 and to inform the alternatives conversation this hearing is meant to open.

## The evidence record on curfews is weak, and the District's own experience should give the Council pause

The Council should not authorize a permanent curfew zone regime without grappling with the evidence that the underlying mechanism does not work and may be counterproductive.

Jennifer Doleac and Jillian Carr studied the District's shift of the general curfew from midnight to 11 p.m. They found that gun violence rose during the newly curfewed hour rather than falling. They proposed two mechanisms, both of which apply to a zone curfew as much as to a citywide one. Curfews thin the streets and remove the bystanders whose ordinary presence deters violence. Curfews also divert police attention from violence prevention toward enforcing the curfew itself.

The Campbell Collaboration's 2016 systematic review pooled twelve quantitative evaluations of juvenile curfew laws and concluded that the pattern of evidence suggests they are ineffective at reducing crime and victimization, with a slight positive effect on crime during the curfew hours themselves. In plain terms: when the available evaluations are aggregated across jurisdictions, curfews do not reduce youth crime, and the pooled estimate points the wrong direction during the very hours the curfew is meant to suppress activity. The Office of Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention's 2024 literature review describes the body of research on newer curfew laws as mixed and thin, and notes long-running concerns about disparate enforcement. The Campbell review supplies the broader evidence base; the Doleac and Carr study supplies the local warning. Together, they counsel against treating curfew authority as an evidence-based public-safety intervention.

Supporters will argue, correctly, that zone curfews are narrower than the citywide curfews most studies examine. That argument cuts the wrong way. If a citywide approach predicated on clearing streets did not reduce violence at the level Doleac and Carr studied, the default assumption should not be that clearing streets more aggressively in smaller geographic zones will succeed where the broader version did not. The Council is being asked to expand a tool whose closest cousin failed in this jurisdiction within recent memory.

I ask the Committee to put this evidence into the hearing record. Future debates over this policy will be poorer if the Doleac and Carr finding is not on the page.

## The behavior is real. The frame is wrong.

Some of these gatherings are chaotic. Some of the conduct is criminal. I am not asking the Council to pretend otherwise. OCA's clients are sometimes among the participants, and their actions sometimes warrant police response.

What I ask the Council to refuse is the slide from "some conduct in some gatherings is criminal" to "youth gathering itself is the public-safety problem." Those are different propositions and they call for different tools. Assault, vandalism, and discharging a firearm in public are already illegal. The Metropolitan Police Department already has authority to address each one. A curfew enforces against a category instead of a behavior. It does not require anyone to be doing anything. It targets who someone is and where they are, without any individual conduct that would otherwise justify a stop. Nothing in this testimony asks MPD to ignore assault, vandalism, gun possession, or violence. The question is whether the District should create a standing offense for youth presence when the conduct that actually threatens public safety is already unlawful.

A racial curfew would be rejected on that ground without a hearing, even if police produced statistics showing the relevant flashpoints were demographically clustered. The age version survives because age is treated as a legitimate basis for this kind of cut in a way race is not. That distinction deserves more scrutiny than it usually receives, particularly when the tool being authorized is standing rather than temporary or emergency.

The disparate enforcement concern is not theoretical. The OJJDP review documents it across decades. The status offense literature documents it across decades. Curfew zone enforcement in the District will fall hardest on Black and poor children. It will fall hardest in neighborhoods where the District's design failures are most acute. Council members supporting B26-0461 should expect that result and be prepared to take responsibility for it.

This expectation should structure the bill rather than be left to post hoc complaint. If the Council enacts any curfew authority, I ask that it require quarterly disaggregated enforcement data, by ward, age, race, enforcement disposition, and subsequent court referral, reported to this Committee in public. Without that requirement, the disparate impact will be visible only to the families absorbing it.

## Federal law restricts what can happen after curfew enforcement

Curfew enforcement is not only a question of whether to make a stop. It is also a question of what happens next. The federal Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention Act (JJDPA) imposes requirements that constrain how the District may handle a curfew-only youth in custody. Any zone curfew authority needs to be designed and overseen with those requirements clearly in view.

Two of the JJDPA's core requirements are most directly relevant.

First, the JJDPA's sight and sound separation requirement prohibits physical, visual, or verbal contact between detained juveniles and adult inmates, except for contact that is brief and inadvertent. Youth cannot share housing areas, bathrooms, recreation spaces, programming areas, or any other detention setting with adults. That requirement is about more than whether staff can manage the risks of mixing. It mandates total separation.

For curfew-only youth, the JJDPA goes a step further. The Deinstitutionalization of Status Offenders requirement, or DSO, generally prohibits placement of status-offense youth in secure detention or locked correctional confinement at all. A curfew violation is a status offense: it is unlawful only because the person is a minor. The same conduct, performed by an adult, would not be unlawful at all. Under DSO, the District should be routing curfew-only youth toward family- and community-based responses, not into locked settings with youth held on delinquency matters, absent a narrow and properly documented exception. The relevant question for any curfew-only youth taken into custody is therefore not how to manage that youth inside a detention setting. The question is why that youth is being brought into a locked detention population in the first place.

The two rules sit in a clear hierarchy. Sight and sound separation is the floor: a separation rule inside detention. DSO is a stronger threshold rule against detention for status-only conduct, designed to keep status-offense youth out of detention populations entirely and to route them toward less harmful responses.

There is a narrow valid-court-order exception under DSO, but it is not a general permission slip to detain curfew youth. It applies only after a valid court order has been issued to a particular youth with due process protections, and then violated. Even then, the JJDPA requires additional findings, consideration of less restrictive alternatives, a release plan, and a time limit generally not to exceed seven days.

I ask the Committee to put these federal requirements into the hearing record alongside the empirical evidence above, and to commit that any curfew authority enacted in the District will be implemented in a manner fully consistent with the JJDPA, including DSO. Relevant authority includes 34 U.S.C. § 11133(a)(11) (the DSO requirement and its valid-court-order provisions) and 34 U.S.C. § 11133(a)(12)–(13) (the separation and jail removal requirements). Accessible summaries are available from the OJJDP Core Requirements page and Juvenile Justice Glossary, the Coalition for Juvenile Justice DSO fact sheet, and the Act4JJ jail removal and sight-and-sound separation fact sheet.

## Emergency authority and permanent authority are different objects

The Council should not conflate two distinct policy questions. A 90-day emergency extension and B26-0461's permanent authority share operative language, but they are different policy objects and they deserve different votes.

A council member can believe that a narrow, time-limited intervention is sometimes justified at a genuine flashpoint and still oppose converting that emergency logic into a permanent standing authority over youth presence. The honest debate is about which tools, for how long, under what guardrails, and paired with what investment. Voting on the two as a single package collapses that debate.

I oppose B26-0461 and am grateful that Chairperson Parker has already voted no. I urge the Committee to use this hearing and the work that follows from it to organize sustained Council opposition to the permanent bill, and to keep that opposition distinct from any future debate over narrower, time-limited tools.

## The hearing title points to the actual answer

The framing of this hearing concedes more than I think the framers intended. It concedes that what young people are doing in these spaces is occupying the only spaces available to them.

The planning argument has been made plainly by local advocates, including Aristotle Theresa writing about Navy Yard: kids "occupy the only spaces available to them" because the District has built neighborhoods full of bars, restaurants, and adult gyms while providing very little actual youth infrastructure. Adults experience the resulting presence as an intrusion into a naturally adult landscape. The landscape is not natural. It was planned, financed, permitted, and zoned around adult tastes and adult spending. Then families were folded back into it, and the children of those families were given very little in return.

Treat that as the policy diagnosis and the curfew reads as a confession of planning failure backed by police power. Treat that as the policy diagnosis and the answer to this hearing's title also becomes available.

Recreation cannot mean only programmed, supervised, ticketed recreation that runs from 4:00 to 7:00 p.m. and then closes. Adolescents need unprogrammed, unsupervised, free, late-evening space where they can gather in groups without an adult holding a clipboard. Opportunity cannot mean only employment programs that train youth for the same adult-consumption economy that excludes them from the corridors in question. Accountability should run in two directions. The Council is rightly asking what the District should expect from young people. The Council should also ask what young people can reasonably expect from the District before being treated as a problem for showing up.

I ask the Council to direct the recreation-and-opportunity investment this hearing contemplates toward unsupervised free youth space in the corridors where the takeover label has been most aggressively deployed, and to do so before any new enforcement authority is enacted. The order matters. If enforcement comes first and infrastructure comes "next year," infrastructure will not come.

## What we are asking

I respectfully ask Chairperson Parker and the Committee to do five things.

First, continue opposing B26-0461 and use the chair of this Committee to organize colleagues toward the same position. The evidence base does not support a permanent curfew zone authority. The disparate enforcement risk is documented and predictable. The design failure that produces these gatherings is not addressed by an enforcement tool.

Second, if curfew authority advances despite the Committee's opposition, support or advance a sunset provision. A sunset clause is harm reduction. It forces the Council to revisit a policy with a thin evidence base rather than allowing emergency logic to harden into standing authority. Chairperson Parker can support such an amendment while continuing to oppose the underlying bill.

Third, require quarterly disaggregated enforcement data, reported publicly to this Committee, by ward, age, race, enforcement disposition, and subsequent court referral. That data should be a precondition for any renewal or expansion.

Fourth, secure on the record the District's commitment that curfew enforcement will comply fully with the JJDPA, including sight and sound separation and the Deinstitutionalization of Status Offenders requirement. Curfew-only youth should not be routed into secure detention populations with delinquency cases, and any use of the narrow valid-court-order exception should be reported to this Committee.

Fifth, direct recreation-and-opportunity investment toward free, late-evening youth space in the corridors most affected by the takeover label, including spaces that do not require a referral, a ticket, a program slot, or constant adult supervision. The hearing title commits this Committee to alternatives. The credibility of that framing depends on those alternatives being funded, sited, and opened before new enforcement authority is made permanent.

A city can misdescribe its own failures by projecting them onto the children forced to live inside those failures. The young people we are now considering curfewing spent their middle school years locked at home for COVID. We told them to stay inside for public health. We are now telling them to stay inside for public safety. The rationales change. The instruction does not. At some point we have to admit that they are part of the public too.

Thank you for the opportunity to submit this testimony. I am happy to provide the underlying studies, additional information, or follow-up to Committee staff at any time.
