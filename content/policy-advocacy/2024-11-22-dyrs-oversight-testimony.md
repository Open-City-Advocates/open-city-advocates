**Subcommittee on Libraries and Youth Affairs, Council of the District of Columbia**  
**Chairperson Zachary Parker**

**Friday, November 22, 2024**

## Testimony of Whitney Louchheim, Co-Founder & Chief Operating Officer

### Oral Testimony

Good morning, Councilmember Parker and Councilmember Frumin. My name is Whitney Louchheim, I am a resident of Ward 6, and I am the Co-Founder and Chief Operating Officer of Open City Advocates, which provides legal representation and holistic advocacy to youth who have been committed to the DC Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services or DYRS.

I have been working with children committed to DYRS for more than 20 years and have represented about 140 young people in the system. I have observed both heartbreaking trauma and the joy that young people experience when they can thrive and grow in their own communities. Today I want to talk about the commitment process and how we have observed it going wrong in the past 18 months.

In our cases, Open City Advocates is typically appointed as defense counsel after the young person is committed to DYRS. Before commitment, DYRS is supposed to have assigned a pre-commitment worker to a young person, completed a needs assessment (the Youth Level of Service, or YLS), and have identified potential placements. That’s what DYRS’s case management handbook says should happen.

Lately, that has not been the process. DYRS rarely completes these tasks prior to the disposition hearing. In some cases, it has taken months for DYRS to assign a Care Coordinator after disposition, and the young person is sitting at the Youth Services Center (YSC) without receiving treatment or services.

I support DYRS’s adoption of the YLS needs assessment, but I have also seen DYRS mishandle that process for children awaiting placement. The YLS involves interviewing the young person, family, and other people in the young person’s life, and looking at any evaluations or records related to the young person. It helps determine their needs and the intensity of services they are supposed to get. Sometimes I’ve seen DYRS not perform the YLS with fidelity, such as interviewing only the child and not the parents or any other family members. In some cases, DYRS has decided on a placement before even completing the YLS.

DYRS assigns each child a “level” of restrictiveness: low, medium, or high. They are then sent to a placement that matches that level in terms of how restrictive the placement is. A low-level placement can be a Child and Family Services Agency foster home or the family home. A medium-level placement can be a group home, DYRS foster home, or an independent living program. These places are not locked but are secured by staff supervision. A high-level placement is a locked, secure facility, such as New Beginnings Youth Development Center, or a psychiatric residential treatment facility or residential treatment center. And though YSC is not a placement, it is the equivalent of a “high” because it’s a locked facility.

DYRS is responsible for identifying an appropriate placement for committed children. This responsibility includes collecting the necessary information from Court Social Services to identify and secure a placement, sending applications to potential placements, developing an individualized treatment plan, and completing any interstate compact paperwork for children they’re sending to out-of-state placements.

The problems with DYRS’s handling of the awaiting placement population at YSC became significantly worse early last year and the awaiting placement period lengthened. The YLS is not being done by the time of commitment, and staff cover multiple roles without the proper credentials or training. For example, Care Coordinators are supposed to act as social workers and case managers and are the primary person responsible for a young person’s treatment plan, placement decisions, and services. Yet we have seen both pre-commitment caseworkers and team meeting facilitators stepping in as Care Coordinators, sometimes for months. DYRS is also required to develop individualized treatment plans for children who are committed, but I have never seen them do that.

In the past, the wait time to get placed would have been a month after commitment at most. Even then, a month is a long time for a child to spend at YSC, especially considering time spent incarcerated before adjudication, and YSC does not offer consistent or in-depth services or programming.

Five to six years ago, by the time of the disposition hearing, application packets to potential placements would have already been sent out and a young person might have already been accepted into a facility. The court used to be able to handle the Article VI hearing (an interstate compact hearing necessary for out-of-state placements) at the same time as disposition, because the young person was already accepted into a facility by then.

Now, it seems like two months after commitment is the minimum wait time. Just getting a Care Coordinator assigned takes weeks or months. I’ve seen youth wait 8 months at YSC following commitment. The average length of commitment is two years, so this means that a child could spend up to a third of the time that a judge has determined necessary for rehabilitation just sitting at YSC without an appropriate placement or individualized treatment. We as a city can do better than that.

I will now turn to my colleague to discuss some ramifications of the current processes. I am available for questions. Thank you.

## Testimony of Joshua Miller, Research & Advocacy Director

### Oral Testimony

Good morning. My name is Joshua Miller and I am the Research & Advocacy Director for Open City Advocates. I have been working with the DC Juvenile Justice Advisory Group over the past year, have served as a liaison to the Youth Leaders in Action subcommittee of the JJAG, and have visited with the YSC Youth Advisory Board for the past three months (including last night). In addition, I have been working on both DC’s 1115 Medicaid Waiver and with the Department of Health Care Finance’s Health System Redesign subcommittee over the past six months to try to get DC prepared for changes in Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services guidance for justice-involved individuals.

To continue the points made by my colleague Whitney, the problems with awaiting placement at the Youth Services Center have significant consequences. During this time, our clients who are awaiting placement are getting quite frustrated and confused. They do not know who to talk to about the delay or where they may be sent. Staff are telling them different things about what placements they might go to, how long they will have to wait, and how long they might spend at placements. They are often changing hands between different DYRS workers without much warning or coordination, which weakens trust. We often have to do a lot of digging and pushing to figure out what is going on.

There is very little transparency throughout the process of securing a placement. We don’t always know when they’re doing the YLS. In some cases, it might be getting done during the awaiting placement time, but other times the YLS isn't completed until after the placement is already decided. DYRS might also be having Placement Review Committee meetings, which are closed-door meetings where they decide the level of placement before packets to those placements get sent out. But staff won’t let attorneys, family, or even the young person attend the meetings. They also don’t tell us what happened at those meetings, so we don’t know what they’re relying on to make a decision.

When the Family Court commits a youth to DYRS custody, the intention is to provide the youth with rehabilitative services and supervision for the duration of the commitment. The longer the youth spends awaiting placement at YSC, the less time they spend engaging in rehabilitation during the course of their commitment.

The effect of these long placement times is terrible on young people. There are a lot of lockdowns, violence, and short staffing at YSC. Those three things mean that kids are locked in their cells often, which in itself causes trauma and mental health problems. They also miss out on a lot of school time because of these lockdowns and short staffing. Often education proceeds with the distribution of paper packets rather than in-person instruction. They don’t get to go to the gym much–we hear reports of less than once per week–and their movement is limited. For kids who are already experiencing the effects of trauma and have mental health needs (which is most of them), this results in them decompensating significantly.

There are minimal mental health services at YSC: some children have short check-ins and unruly group sessions approximately weekly and one psychiatrist for about one hundred children. They’re not doing evidence-based therapies. Kids aren’t getting the mental health services they need this way.

Their mental health is deteriorating in part because they’re not being told why they are still at YSC or they’re getting conflicting stories from staff. Young people are basically in a state of duress by the time their placement is decided. It wears on them. They feel that it’s "dead time" to be at YSC, because that time does not count toward any future placement’s timeline. For example, OCA clients have been told that their placement is New Beginnings, which they did not want to go to because of safety concerns or mental health needs, but they are still willing to go because, at that point, they’ll go anywhere to start their time and programming. And it is rare that OCA attorneys challenge DYRS’s decision to send a child out of state, even if it’s an inappropriate placement or too far from family, because the client just wants to get out of YSC. In these cases, clients will say “just send me.”

To summarize, the awaiting placement delays at YSC are causing significant trauma and harm for youth who are committed to DYRS. Their mental health decompensates, their education is stymied, and they are unable to engage in normal adolescent experiences such as bonding with family and community. This has become an urgent issue for youth committed to DYRS. I’m happy to answer any questions on this or other aspects of our written testimony or my experience.

## Joint Written Testimony

*Testimony of Co-Founder & Chief Operating Officer Whitney Louchheim, Esq. and Research & Advocacy Director Joshua Miller, PhD*

### Who We Are

Open City Advocates (OCA) is a small organization with a big impact. We model an innovative solution in juvenile defense in Washington, DC, and nationwide. Founded to address the racial inequities and unjust incarceration practices in DC’s juvenile system, OCA provides youth-centered legal defense and holistic advocacy for young people after sentencing in the juvenile system.

### Who We Serve

All of our clients have been committed to the care and custody of DYRS by DC’s Family Court. “Commitment” to DYRS means that a young person is a ward of the city, oftentimes until age 21, and that their placement may change several times with little or no court oversight—including placement at home or in a carceral facility.

There are extreme racial disparities in the youth delinquency system nationwide, with Black and Latino/a children being committed and incarcerated at 4.7 times the rate compared to their white peers.¹ It is even more extreme in the District of Columbia, where 100% of all committed children are children of color, and 93% are Black, while Black youth comprise only 51% of the DC youth population.²

OCA’s clients have had extensive contact with the delinquency system; most have also been in DC’s child welfare system, and all are at high risk for entering the DC Jail or being harmed or killed by street violence. The majority of our clients hail from DC’s Wards 7 and 8, where 35% of children live in poverty.³ Over the last two years, 46% of violent crimes committed with guns in the city occurred in these two wards.⁴ In our 19-year history, we have lost six clients to gun violence and one to a drug overdose. OCA works to help our clients envision and actualize a healthy adulthood instead of accepting death or incarceration by age 25.

### 1. Recent and Ongoing Staff Assaults and Cultural Issues

In May, our team testified before Councilmember Trayon White’s subcommittee about staff assaults on youth, using as an example a lawsuit brought by a client of Disability Rights DC that he experienced both abuse and retaliation for reporting that abuse. Since then, we’ve continued to observe similar behavior. That testimony is repeated here in part, but we are sorry to report that we have observed that staff at DYRS are not following their own de-escalation procedures and are using violence even when their own policies don’t allow it. Staff are even dragging, pushing, and forcing youth into areas without  cameras to carry out these assaults. Even with the lawsuit and increased attention, this behavior has continued. We believe this is an ongoing staff training and culture issue.

Following on the heels of a month of lockdowns at YSC in 2023, Disability Rights DC’s report details the terrible violence that their client endured at the hands of DYRS staff at New Beginnings. DYRS staff also allegedly retaliated against the young person for filing a complaint. Unfortunately, based on our clients’ experiences, I can say with confidence that this was not an isolated incident.

DRDC also reports that none of the staff misconduct clearly visible in video was named in DYRS’s internal incident report. It’s not surprising that staff present incidents in a way that show their actions in the best possible light, but it’s part of a pattern that makes it likely that no one, including DC Council or even DYRS leadership, is learning the full extent of staff abuses within DYRS facilities.

As the lawsuit demonstrates, if an incident that includes staff abuse results in an official incident report, it will likely exclude the staff wrongdoing. We have seen this many times. For example, we have heard details from our clients about unnecessary physical aggression on many occasions, and when we requested the incident reports, there was never any mention of staff wrongdoing. Staff also know all the spots where there is no camera coverage, something our clients have repeatedly told us and that is also brought up in this recent lawsuit.

You might think that young people will fill in these gaps in their grievances and by speaking with attorneys to file lawsuits. However, youth often do not report staff wrongdoing out of fear of retaliation. Consider that DYRS’s grievance process (known as Project Hands) goes to DYRS’s Office of Internal Integrity. As DYRS has explained it, when OII receives a complaint, they go to the Supervising YDR and ask them about it. This means that complaints about staff wrongdoing go directly back to supervisors and from there to involved staff. The potential for retaliation is very high.

In fact, we have had clients who have experienced such retaliation. After submitting complaints about some of the same assaultive behavior detailed in the current lawsuit, they have experienced retaliation by other staff such as meals being withheld or rumors circulating about them being a “snitch.”

What about attorneys? As lawyers, we cannot share allegations of staff misconduct without our clients’ consent. Our clients generally do not give their consent, because they are almost universally afraid of retaliation and of being labeled a “snitch.” And we cannot truthfully advise them that they will not suffer retaliation—our obligation is to protect their interests as they express them to us, and they rarely think that the risks of retaliation are worth the benefits of potential staff accountability.

What about oversight? Unfortunately, if our oversight agencies are getting their information about staff misconduct only from official incident reports and grievances, then they are not getting the full story. We need oversight that will also affirmatively seek out and interview committed youth, rather than waiting for incidents to come to them.

Even if such oversight does interview youth, it will still be difficult 1) for them to get youth to confide in them, and 2) for them to actually ensure that those youth do not experience retaliation.

### 2. The Commitment Model

The District’s unique approach to juvenile commitment requires significant unpacking, in part because of unfamiliar terminology and a set of values prioritizing youth well-being, family reunification, and holistic and rehabilitative treatment that is often not reflected in DYRS’s actions. In particular, DYRS is mandated by law to achieve its preventive and rehabilitative goals in “the least restrictive setting necessary, with a preference at all times for the preservation of the family and the integration of parental, guardian, and custodial accountability and participation in treatment and counseling programs.”⁵ It’s hard to overemphasize how often this “least restrictive setting” requirement is violated, or how often parents are unduly burdened in their efforts to maintain contact with their children or to exercise their parental rights.

Though this is not always the case in other states, in DC the neglect and delinquency systems are divided between two different agencies. There is frequent crossover, with some youth actively dual-jacketed with cases in both systems, and more youth with inactive cases in one system but active ones in the other. In a more fundamental sense, juvenile justice in DC is founded on the notion that delinquency is always the outgrowth of caregiver and systemic neglect: this is why young people are not “found guilty” but “adjudicated involved” and they are not sentenced to a term of incarceration or other punishment, but put on probation or “committed.”

Young people who are adjudicated involved in delinquent acts can be put on probation under the supervision of the Court Social Services Division (CSSD), which is a federally-funded branch of the DC Superior Court, or committed to the “supervision, custody, and care” of DYRS. DYRS effectively takes over many parental rights and responsibilities (though parents of committed children do not cede crucial medical or education rights for their children).

The decision whether to place children on probation or commitment is made by judges after adjudication and upon the review of a precommitment report/recommendation that is supposed to be prepared by DYRS Care Coordinators. Most children receive probation, or are diverted even earlier, and indeed, as far as we are aware, this has happened for all white children since we began working in the system more than two decades ago. The court is expected to recommend specific services, treatment modalities, and supervision requirements, but cannot specify a facility. CSSD provides some initial information for this review, often including health insurance status and psychological reports that DYRS relies on in its own assessments. However, without oversight of CSSD, the District is not able to guarantee that the assessments and other information provided by them is always complete and accurate. Thus DYRS can and should perform its own evaluations.

If children are committed to DYRS, judges have fewer opportunities for oversight from that point forward. The child’s placement and services are almost entirely within the purview of DYRS. The agency, and not the judge, will then decide whether to lock the child up in secure detention facilities, whether to put them in a group home or send them out of state, and also whether and when they’re ready to be sent home. It is usually a combination of all the above over the course of years, and there is limited access to the courts after the point of commitment.

### 3. Reentry Planning

If a child’s commitment requires them to complete a program at New Beginnings, they can expect to move through seven levels of programming. DYRS usually reports this as six levels of positive youth development, in part because it does not have specific programming for the final level, but DYRS has added a significant extra step because of its own failure to begin discharge planning in a timely fashion. Historically, discharge from New Beginnings was supposed to happen within a week or two of reaching this sixth level, but we have observed that as the delays mount, kids become frustrated. DYRS thus added a seventh step: petitioning for release, known as “home petition.” This has grown from a two-week process into an extra forty-four days, at least: DYRS now requires that a child remain at Level 6 for thirty days, successfully petition for the next level which is called “home petition,” and then DYRS will start the process of scheduling a discharge meeting after two weeks of maintaining good behavior on home petition. (And at that meeting they are likely to learn of obstacles to discharge that may require additional days and weeks of incarceration.) In practice, this means that a process that should be as short as six months in the best case scenario now requires between nine and eighteen months, and almost always longer than a year.

Much of this extra time is generated by the bureaucratic delays caused by waiting so long to begin discharge planning. School enrollment, identifying foster homes or ensuring adequate space and safety in the family home, lining up community-based providers for referrals, and completing all the paperwork takes time. Moreover, as children spend longer and longer feeling that they have completed all the expectations set for them without the promised reward of freedom, it gets harder for some of them to maintain positive behavior, which can lead to further delays that are then blamed on them: indeed, they see this temporizing as a violation of the implicit contract the program promises.

In our view, Level 4, dubbed “Independence,” is the right time to start reentry and discharge planning, and according to DYRS’s most recent Care Coordination Handbook from 2017, this is precisely when discharge planning is supposed to begin. Sadly, DYRS no longer follows this discharge planning timeline. If they’re going to struggle, kids most often stall at the start of the program, stuck at level 1 or 2. Thus a child who has completed level 4 is likely to complete the rest of the program on schedule, is two or three months away from completing the program, and is ready to begin concrete planning for a return to the community. But that assumes a six-level system. In his testimony on November 18, 2024, Director Abed suggested that reentry planning should occur at Level 5, which is about the same amount of time earlier than release in the new seven-level system and would likely be enough time under the new system. Unfortunately, that’s not what we have observed so far.

In any case, the cost of making last-minute decisions with uncertain timelines is that neither schools nor community placements can keep spots open indefinitely.

### 4. Extending the Medicaid Continuum of Care

While incarcerated youth have generally been excluded from Medicaid eligibility, a recent change to the federal appropriation law requires state Medicaid and Children’s Health Insurance Programs to provide both adults and young people in carceral and juvenile justice agencies certain screenings, diagnostic services, case management, and referrals 30 days before release and 30 days after discharge. It also allows states to apply for a waiver to expand those services up to 90 days before and after discharge. The mandated expansion of Medicaid-covered services—especially prevention, screening, and wellness services—will have a salutary effect on young people in the District. There is also good reason to hope that Medicaid-funded reentry support by community-based providers would create a continuum of care that begins during incarceration under the 1115 waiver process. At the same time, we have serious concerns about the capacity of our behavioral health system to meet the needs of incarcerated youth, and we worry that DYRS may not be prepared to implement these new services with an attention to access and quality.

The District of Columbia is required to create an internal operational plan before January 1, 2025, that describes how it will meet the following mandates and requirements:⁶

1. Screening and diagnostic services, including “any screenings and diagnostic services which meet reasonable standards of medical and dental practice, as determined by the state, or as otherwise indicated as medically necessary, in accordance with the Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment (EPSDT) requirements, including a behavioral health screening or diagnostic services.”⁷ This includes immunizations.
2. Targeted case management, including
   1. “Comprehensive assessment and periodic reassessment of individual needs, to determine the need for any medical, educational, social, or other services;
   2. Development (and periodic revision) of a specific person-centered care plan based on the information collected through the assessment;
   3. Referral and related activities (such as scheduling appointments for the individual) to help the eligible individual obtain needed services, including activities that help link the individual with medical, social, and educational providers or other programs and services that are capable of providing needed services to address identified needs and achieve goals specified in the care plan; and
   4. Monitoring and follow-up activities, including activities and contacts that are necessary to ensure that the care plan is effectively implemented and adequately addresses the needs of the eligible individual and which may be with the individual, family members, service providers, or other entities or individuals and conducted as frequently as necessary, and including at least one annual monitoring.”⁸
3. Referrals to community-based providers of behavioral health services “in the geographic area where the eligible juvenile will be residing upon release, whenever possible.”⁹
4. A “warm handoff” for any new case manager that includes a meeting between the young person and both case managers and a review of the care plan and steps to ensure continuity.¹⁰

Beyond the requirements, DC has also submitted a request for waiver under Medicaid’s 1115 rule, which would allow an extension of these required services from 90 days before release and 90 days afterwards. This is particularly important since it would allow significant overlap from community-based providers, and would absorb some of the costs associated with this enhanced level of care.

However, one crucial element of youth commitment to DYRS is that, as we have described, there is rarely clarity about when, exactly, a period of confinement in a secure facility will end. This indeterminacy is tied to the nature of the behavioral modifications required for advancement and release, and the limited supply of community placements. For committed youth at YSC awaiting a community-based placement, release dates are driven by the availability of placements and other logistics. For youth at New Beginnings, release dates are based on completion of programming and appropriate planning by the Care Coordination team, which can vary widely.

These changes to what is allowed and required by CMS  present an opportunity for a significant improvement in the operation of DYRS reentry planning. This new Medicaid mandate requires just such clarity to determine when a young person will begin to qualify for Medicaid-subsidized release services. Giving children and the Medicaid system a clear and credible release date will thus require a review of Team Decision Meeting processes, the New Beginnings discharge process, and the implementation of significant case management changes. If this were implemented honestly, it would be severely underutilized since the final steps of release planning are often done only days or even hours before a child is released despite constant pressure from advocates to start discharge planning earlier. There is also a risk that only small tweaks to the current system would lead to serious overutilization, with release dates constantly pushed back after 90 days of Medicaid services have been consumed. Thus, in order to effectively implement this waiver, discharge planning will need to begin upon a youth's entry into a facility. This planning should include an anticipated or expected release date in order to maximize the utilization and benefit of the waiver.

Practically, DYRS must review their discharge planning processes and make the necessary changes to their policies relating to team decision-making meetings and case management. A change in these practices is desperately needed to allow for the waiver to be effective and to provide discharge planning with fidelity for youth.

While the District recently authored Emergency Intensive Care Coordination (ICC) Regulations providing for High Fidelity Wraparound model services to address the lack of intensive services for youth, the regulations failed to clearly address the eligibility determination process for ICC services, the fidelity of ICC services, and the transparency of program evaluation. Given the historic concerns, policy changes through emergency regulations or the 1115 waivers do not change the ineffective foundation of service delivery on which these new policies are based.

High-Fidelity Wraparound is an evidence-informed, strength-based process “led by a Care Coordinator who is responsible for collaborating with a team-based planning process where representatives of multiple systems come together with a child, youth, [sic], and their family to create a highly individualized plan to address the complex emotional needs of a child or youth.”¹¹ Yet there is not adequate provision of High-Fidelity Wraparound in the District, despite the fact that High-Fidelity Wraparound services are associated with high rates of success in the community in other jurisdictions, including massive reductions in recidivism, ongoing sustained housing, and major improvements in school attendance. From our experience, providers are already struggling to fill positions and maintain capacity to properly serve youth and adults. Unfortunately, the proposal to use current providers will further strain a system already suffering from staffing deficiencies resulting in fragmented mental health services.

What’s more, there are also significant gaps in the community-based treatment system: for instance, there is currently only one provider in the District certified in the multisystem therapy modality which is particularly useful for system-impacted youth aged 12-17, is “desperately need,” but the one provider cannot meet demand.¹² As well, there are reportedly no programs in the District that take Medicaid and also provide access to monthly injectable buprenorphine, which has become an essential tool for opioid addiction treatment in other states. This means that District residents with opioid use disorders who rely on Medicaid do not have a single community provider that can serve them.

In its performance oversight testimony, the DC Department of Behavioral Health (DBH) usually pegs the capacity for community-based High-Fidelity Wraparound services at 85 young people. In FY18, DBH reported an average time from enrollment to treatment of 33 days for children in the community. For FY23, they reported an average time from referral to intake appointment of 19 days, and an average time from intake to diagnostic assessment of 46 days, for a total of 65 days pre-treatment. They were unable to report time to treatment. This suggests a behavioral health system that is quickly falling behind under various staffing pressures.

Increased Medicaid funding can certainly help alleviate one pressure, but 1115 demonstrations are supposed to be cost neutral, suggesting a reliance on preventative gains and a shift from more expensive and intensive providers in carceral settings to more efficient and effective providers in the community. In order to achieve these gains, we will need to ensure that the capacity of community-based providers, which is currently at a breaking point, is expanded to meet the current and future needs of incarcerated young people seeking release.
