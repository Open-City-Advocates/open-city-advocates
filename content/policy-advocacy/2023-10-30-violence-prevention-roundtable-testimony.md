**Joint Public Oversight Roundtable on Violence Reduction Programs in the District**

**Committee on the Judiciary and Public Safety, Committee of the Whole, and Committee on Executive Administration and Labor, Council of the District of Columbia**

**Monday, October 30, 2023**

Written and presented by Joshua A. Miller, PhD on behalf of Open City Advocates

Good morning Chairman Mendelson and Chairwoman Pinto, Councilmembers Bonds, Lewis George, Henderson, Allen, and friends,

My name is Joshua Miller. I am the Research & Advocacy Director for [Open City Advocates](https://www.opencityadvocates.org/): we provide post-disposition representation for children after their commitment to DYRS, and we've been disheartened by recent changes in the ways that children are treated by the criminal legal system. I am also a Ward 4 resident. This testimony aims to share our views of the recent efforts to address crime in the district, and request that you consider alternatives to the proposals that currently aim to reduce civil liberties and increase incarceration.

With MPD's recent announcement that DC has [passed 200 murders](https://www.washingtonpost.com/dc-md-va/2023/09/26/dc-homicides-increase-crime-crisis/) this year, it is obvious to most people that something has gone wrong. Something that worked for a while in our city has broken down: in 2012 there were only 88 homicides. Yet I urge you to resist the handbook from the 1990s that led DC to have one of the highest crime and incarceration rates in the country.

### Super-predator Rhetoric and a Historic Mistake

In the 1990s, our city helped launch the rhetoric of Princeton political scientist John Dilulio, whose theory of the super-predator claimed that young men were "fatherless, Godless, and jobless": they had too little supervision, too much selfishness, and too little hope for the future. He predicted that youth crime would triple in the next decade. But the "army of young male predatory street criminals" he prophesied never arrived—he made his predictions just as the crime wave crested and began to recede. Yet fearing these prophecies, we responded to unprecedented violence (almost 500 murders a year here) by instituting a series of tough-on-crime policies that overcrowded DC's Lorton prison facility and helped force DC to the brink of bankruptcy. Curfews were instituted, a move later [promoted by President Clinton](https://www.nytimes.com/1996/05/31/us/politics-juvenile-curfews-clinton-backs-plan-to-deter-youthful-violence.html). Pretrial detention in [dangerous facilities](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/local/1995/12/06/occoquan-called-districts-most-violent-prison/9effc264-7942-436b-ac78-bf1620662d87/) was [expanded](https://perma.cc/8VKJ-2Q44): we eliminated cash bail but created a rebuttable presumption to detention for many crimes, boosting the pretrial detention rate from 2% to 15% where it has remained. And police wasted time and goodwill using random street stops to find guns and drugs.

In recent months, DC Council has been evaluating legislation that makes it easier to detain young people before trial, increases sentences for gun possession, and allows random public searches of many justice-involved individuals even without a conviction, after announcing a new policy of localized curfew enforcement despite rigorous empirical evidence from DC's own Shot Spotter that our city's curfews [likely increases crime](https://direct.mit.edu/rest/article-abstract/100/4/609/58496/Keep-the-Kids-Inside-Juvenile-Curfews-and-Urban?redirectedFrom=fulltext) (shootings increased in a way that couldn't be explained as merely shifting crime earlier) and evidence from countless cities that [curfew stops and arrests disproportionately affect Black and Latino children](https://www.sentencingproject.org/app/uploads/2022/10/Disproportionate-Minority-Contact-in-the-Juvenile-Justice-System.pdf). The parallels to the 1990s are clear, but these prescriptions are predicated on a mistaken diagnosis.

### Stop and Frisk is Not the Answer

It seems obvious that the collective goal of all these policies is to give law enforcement the ability to stop, search, and punish people in possession of illegal guns, perhaps because we fear that DC cannot stem the pipeline of illegal weapons into the city any other way. The proposed provisions for random public searches of those on pretrial detention are one example: while there is no way to determine immediately that someone is on pretrial detention, this provision would retroactively legitimate some searches that would otherwise be unconstitutional, and whose evidence would therefore be excluded. It would be better by far to focus on developing reasonable suspicion before a stop in the first place, however. Increasing opportunities for both searches and frisks is not going to have the intended effect, and this policy will not make our communities safer while it will exacerbate tensions between police and the communities that experience enhanced stop-and-frisk levels.

The legal term for stop-and-frisk is a ["Terry Stop"](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/terry_stop/stop_and_frisk) from Terry v. Ohio, but ordinary people recognize it as a random search. Police officers stop people—overwhelmingly young Black men—for minor infractions or merely to request information, and use the encounter as an excuse to check their clothing and effects for weapons or drugs. For the Supreme Court in 1968, the pat down was justified in terms of officer safety—questions asked "might be answered with a bullet"—even though the department encourages the officer only put themself into this position of danger in order to perform the pat down. Such are the ironies of Constitutional law.

In his book on DC's unique history of mass incarceration, Locking up Our Own, Yale law professor James Forman, Jr. [recounts](https://www.amazon.com/Locking-Up-Our-Own-Punishment/dp/0374189978) the way that students at Maya Angelou Charter School would be met by police officers on their lunch break in the 1990s:

> "When the police rushed onto our corner, our students were forced to 'assume the position,' with their legs spread, faces against the wall or squad car, and hands behind their heads. Then they were searched, with the officers feeling every inch of their bodies, leaving the sidewalks strewn with notebooks, broken pencils, lipstick, and combs. Not once, over the course of about ten searches, did the police recover anything illegal."

But it takes a lot of such indiscriminate stops to find a gun. A [review of NYPD stop-and-frisk data](https://www.nyclu.org/en/stop-and-frisk-data) in 2015 showed that guns were only discovered about once every thousand times a person was stopped this way. That is why courts are increasingly treating these stops as unreasonable.

Treating our young people as preternaturally aggressive is not going to work to reduce crime. Jailing them without trial is not going to work. Curfews are not going to work. Random searches are not going to work. And the city's police, prosecutors, and corrections officers will just end up exhausted as a result. So, what's the alternative?

### Alternatives: Violence Interruption as Focused Deterrence

DC's own Juvenile Justice Advisory Group has [made many recommendations](https://ovsjg.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/ovsjg/service_content/attachments/JJAG%20PINS%20Alternatives%20Report%20February%202020.pdf), as has the [National Institute for Criminal Justice Reform](https://cjcc.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/cjcc/page_content/attachments/DC%20Violence%20Reduction%20Strategic%20Plan%20-%20April%202022.pdf)–many of which would be preventative in the long term. Importantly they emphasize that when we are seeking to enforce rules that only apply on the basis of age—status offences—we should use non-law-enforcement means: rather than police arrest and delivery to a youth jail, we should use social workers and non-carceral safe spaces. They also praise the efforts of violence interrupters, who are a cost-effective alternative to sworn police officers who are also in short supply. The Cure the Streets Program has long been shown to reduce targeted criminal behavior like gun violence through a mixture of focused deterrence and creating prosocial alternative investments of mentoring time and programmatic resources in those most at risk. While much of the rhetoric around Cure the Streets focuses on violence as a "contagion" and uses public health models to identify at-risk populations, interrupt the spread, and "cure" root causes, in practice violence interrupters use traditional conflict resolution and youth mentoring approaches and this veneer of medical authority is unhelpful. We can readily see the disanalogies after seeing a real epidemic in action, and the nationwide outcomes for such programs have been mixed, with arguments about model fidelity used to try to explain away poor showings in some cities. A more important question is whether violence interruption strategies should be done in collaboration with police and prosecutors or resist collaboration with the parts of government that have the power to arrest and incarcerate. In my view, we should supercharge collaboration.

For police, the evidence supports a targeted combination of carrots and sticks. A nearby model for this is what police did in High Point, NC. [The High Point Drug Market Intervention](https://crimesolutions.ojp.gov/ratedprograms/361#pd) targeted an open-air drug market in a middle class African-American community. They quietly built cases against entire crews, then invited "VIPs" from those crews (and their attorneys) to a presentation of the cases against them. At these "call-ins" they offered a choice: either end the retail drug trade in High Point and receive significant support for the transition to civilian life (the carrot) or go to prison with the evidence already assembled (the stick). Not only did the drug market close for good, but violence in the neighborhood went down 57%--and it stayed down. This is not to say that drug use permanently ended in High Point—evidence suggests it moved to the motels near the highway—but it was no longer transacted in an open-air drug market with significant costs to the surrounding community, and violence overall decreased. Similar programs focused on firearms have had great success in [Boston](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/188741.pdf) and [Chicago](https://idoc.illinois.gov/content/dam/soi/en/web/idoc/reportsandstatistics/documents/READI-Jan2023.pdf).

Chief Smith has [already signaled](https://www.nbcwashington.com/news/local/dc-names-next-police-chief-pamela-smith/3385962/) that she's interested in "long-term investigations focused on the most violent people." But we have not yet heard about plans to organize such "call-ins," and without the right support from all branches of government, police departments and prosecutors will tend to develop cases, prosecute them, and incarcerate everyone they can. Given that this tool of focused deterrence is available and as-yet unused, and that it can be a significant cost savings as well as preventing another bout of mass incarceration in the District, we believe that it is incumbent on Council to promote and support such alternative models.

We can be reasonably confident that severe punishment is no deterrent at all to young people with very short time horizons or expectations for the future. Certainty trumps severity. But even the High Point model won't be nearly as effective without the carrot: supportive reentry to civilian life. The Office of Neighborhood Safety and Engagement's [People of Promise](https://onse.dc.gov/service/people-promise#:%7E:text=District%20residents%20who%20have%20been,opportunities%20that%20can%20help%20them.) program was designed to identify those folks who are likely to be perpetrators or victims of violence, and accelerate service delivery to them so they can transition out of the street violence. Yet the office that could potentially lead the "whole-of-government" approach we've been promised has been without a permanent director since February. Perhaps this is why the list of individuals to whom the People of Promise program had hoped to direct such resources is not being continually updated, and too many DC residents who know likely perpetrators and victims of violence are unable to get their loved ones and neighbors the resources they need to make a different choice. In this, the OAG's program is demonstrably more effective.

### Conclusion

So far, we have only heard about policing, prosecutions, and prison sentences. "Prevention" and "punishment" are being treated as severable processes. In truth, they are best understood holistically, and developed through interagency cooperation. A true whole-of-government approach would incorporate the schools, housing, employment, entrepreneurship, behavioral health, parks and recreation, etc. It takes a lot of work to coordinate across agencies–especially when some of those agencies are federal—but it's worth doing right.

We can do better than the failed policies of the past. We can do better than lockdowns, lock-ins, and lock-ups. Our children are not super-predators: let's make DC an open city for all.

Please contact me at [joshua@opencityadvocates.org](mailto:joshua@opencityadvocates.org) if you have further questions.
