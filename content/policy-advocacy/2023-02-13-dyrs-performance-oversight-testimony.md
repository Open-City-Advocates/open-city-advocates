**Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services Performance Oversight Hearing**

**Committee on Recreation, Libraries, and Youth Affairs, Council of the District of Columbia**

**Chairman White and Members of the Committee**

**Monday, February 13, 2023**

## Testimony of Penelope Spain, CEO & Co-Founder

### Oral Testimony

Good afternoon, Chairman White, Councilwoman Bonds, and members of the Committee. My name is Penelope Spain and I am a resident of Ward 4 and the CEO and Co-Founder of Open City Advocates, which provides legal representation and mentoring to youth who have been committed to DYRS. Open City Advocates is also a member of the Youth Justice Project Coalition, [a coalition of DC-based organizations that strives to create a DC where our children, and particularly our Black and Brown children, are free to be kids and are supported throughout their transformation into healthy, thriving adults.]

Under Director Cairns, DYRS made laudable efforts to improve case planning for young people, with an emphasis on serving them in the community instead of resorting to harmful practices of incarceration. First, DYRS is replacing its out-dated, deficit-based systems of risk assessment and case planning with the more rigorous, strength-based Youth Level of Service (YLS). The YLS addresses both risk assessment and service planning, is instrumental in reducing reliance on cookie-cutter services, and instead emphasizes the importance of individualizing services and supports to meet the unique needs of each and every child at a particular moment in their adolescence and personal growth. Importantly, the YLS has been statistically proven to reduce recidivism. It can accurately distinguish low-risk from high-risk youth, and designs effective, individualized case planning. The YLS is indeed a rigorous tool that takes time and effort to implement; our children are very much worth that time and effort. Not only will the use of the YLS lead to better outcomes for children, it will also lead to a safer community.

Second, DYRS leadership is working diligently to improve the methods by which its Care Coordinators support young people by encouraging more frequent, in-person contact with youth both in facilities and in the community, which came nearly to a halt during the pandemic, and by ensuring warm hand-offs when referring youth to community-based services. Meeting with young people face-to-face can be time intensive for Care Coordinators. But again, are our children worth any less? At Open City Advocates, we have seen this in our own experience. Since the pandemic, DYRS has been holding most of its Team Decision Making Meetings over the phone. It is high time for DYRS to revert to in-person team meetings. Our clients who receive only phone meetings feel disconnected from and disregarded by DYRS. In sharp contrast, the few clients who have received in-person meetings feel a sense of trust and care from DYRS, and are, in turn, more responsive to DYRS services.

DYRS must, however, stay laser-focused on the youth whom the DC Family Court has committed to its care and custody. Although we applaud DYRS's efforts to open its Achievement Centers and community-based programs to youth who are not committed, we must be sure that our city does not come to rely on DYRS as the agency tasked with preventing youth violence more generally. For example, the budding Violence Prevention Program being developed under DYRS – a program that targets "at-risk" 10-14 year-olds – mistakenly tasks DYRS with a project that falls squarely outside its purview. Our Coalition strongly urges the Mayor and DC Council to relocate that program within the Department of Human Services, which runs DC's other youth diversion and prevention programs. DHS has years of experience developing meaningful intervention services that are therapeutic, trauma-responsive, and effective. Under no circumstances should DYRS run a program that pulls children from underserved schools and introduces them to the city's justice system. We have spoken repeatedly in the District about breaking the school to prison pipeline and yet this program is priming the pipe, widening our net to funnel Black and Brown children into our criminal justice system. From a legal perspective, this is likely to have dire consequences. Children who are struggling do not need to be "scared straight" by a system, city, or program previewing for them what is in store if they don't learn to fly right. We know those programs don't work and instead exacerbate trauma and increase the likelihood of future court contact. These children don't need to be scared straight; they need therapeutic, trauma-responsive services provided by our mental health professionals and recreational specialists, not our juvenile justice agency.

Thank you for the opportunity to testify today. I am available to answer any questions.

### Written Testimony

*Submitted as part of a joint filing by four members of the Youth Justice Project Coalition: the Georgetown Juvenile Justice Clinic & Initiative, Open City Advocates, the Public Defender Service for the District of Columbia, and School Justice Project.*

Unfortunately, those failures have cultivated in our youth a great sense of distrust in our systems and the people claiming to help. It is only by consistent, in-person connection that we can begin to rebuild that trust. Without it, our youth committed to DYRS will feel only the cold shoulder of phone calls, finger-wagging, and referrals to unknown services. But with trust built through in-person, individualized care, young people can come to see that our city is full of individuals in their communities who wish to lift them up and rich with services opening their doors to them.
