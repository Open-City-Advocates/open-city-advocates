**Child Behavioral Health Services Dashboard Act of 2024**

**July 11, 2024**

**Committee on Health**

**Councilmember Christina Henderson, Chairperson**

**Testimony of Joshua A. Miller, PhD**

**Research & Advocacy Director, Open City Advocates**

Good morning. My name is Joshua Miller. I am the Research & Advocacy Director for Open City Advocates, which provides legal representation and holistic advocacy to youth who have been committed to DYRS. I am here today to testify regarding the Child Behavioral Health Services Dashboard Act of 2024.

Robust community-based behavioral health services must be a vital part of any successful juvenile justice system, and the lack of access to such services is a major reason why ours is currently failing. Inadequate provision of services in the community means that more children turn to substances and delinquency, it means that more of the children who are committed to DYRS languish in secure facilities like YSC decompensating rather than returning to school and the community to receive the services they need to flourish, and it means that more of our children end up shipped out of state to treatment facilities that are unnecessarily carceral at a much higher cost and much lower efficacy than what we could do in the community.

This is a human crisis, but it's also a fiscal one. Our overreliance on secure facilities for children adjudicated delinquent can be directly tied to the fact that our community-based services are overwhelmed. Building and staffing these secure facilities then weighs down our budgets at a much higher cost than similar services in our neighborhoods, and there is a mountain of evidence that incarceration is deeply harmful and non-rehabilitative for children.

I have been working in carceral and reentry spaces for many years, and I've frequently found that it is difficult for those who most need behavioral healthcare to access it. Those suffering from mental health crises are asked to tackle gargantuan bureaucratic hurdles, and when they fail their failure is often criminalized. It requires an absurd amount of expertise and an actively-maintained database to find services for people in need.

Although it's a little absurd that we need legislation to create a website, we *do* need the dashboard described by this bill, and we apparently do need this legislation to order the interagency cooperation required to make this dashboard a reality and to support other efforts like the 211 Warmline.

Nonetheless, this bill and the dashboard it provides can help visualize the gap between the need and the provision of services, and empowers DMHHS to coordinate across agencies and with private providers. It will give Council, DBH, and providers a real-time sense of the desperate needs going unfulfilled.

## Recommendations

### 1. Don't replicate existing resources, but do build a "No Wrong Door" system

The "directory" provision of this bill seems to suggest that the dashboard should replicate existing databases of services. Currently the DBH Network of Care and the DC Health FindHelp sites provide a good database of providers in the District. The goal should be to get real time reports on systemic accountability measures like available beds and time to treatment, and to provide case managers and providers with information about where capacity exists for referrals. This will help save those seeking services from having to diligently call every provider only to be turned away or ignored again and again. Think of this as a digital adjunct to the 211 Warmline and Access Helpline.

### 2. Clarify the type of beds available for inpatient services

This legislation currently requires DBH to provide real-time information on "The number of beds available for behavioral health inpatient treatment services in the District." But not all beds are created equally. What treatment modalities does the institution provide? Does the institution accept children who are justice-involved? These and other such questions should be addressed in order for the dashboard to be maximally useful.

### 3. Institute a No Reject, No Eject Policy for all DBH-contracted providers of psychiatric facilities

Some DC providers have refused to treat young people who are justice involved. One state that has dealt with this tension effectively is New Jersey. Providers who accept Medicaid must implement a no-refusal intake policy so that no young person is ever turned away from the care they need, and that no matter how difficult things get they are still entitled to assistance. We recommend that DC implement a similar No Reject, No Eject policy for all DBH-contracted providers of psychiatric services.

### 4. Implement Collective Impact to create a culture of ongoing improvement around access

DC needs to implement a Collective Impact-style model that empowers and funds a backbone organization to coordinate and convene public and private stakeholders around shared goals. In an ideal District government, there'd really be no reason for this bill to exist. Everyone involved can readily see the need for this coordination and a dashboard of this sort, and they ought to be able to create a website to house the relevant data. The main obstacle is data sharing and oversight, not website design, and the executive branch runs all the relevant agencies. A Collective Impact backbone organization could be the Criminal Justice Coordinating Council, the DC Auditor, an Office of Oversight that collects all the various Ombudspeople together, a university center, or a neutral non-profit. But they should be empowered, nimble, and laser-focused on addressing these urgent problems.

I'm happy to answer any questions, and happier still to work with you towards full home rule in a future that is equitable, fair and compassionate towards our children.
