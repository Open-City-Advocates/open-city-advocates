**Justice-Involved Youth in Community Act of 2025**  
**Committee on the Judiciary and Public Safety, Council of the District of Columbia**  
**Ward 3 Councilmember Brooke Pinto, Chairperson**

**April 23, 2025**

## Testimony of Whitney Louchheim, Co-Founder & Chief Operating Officer

### Oral Testimony

Good morning, Chairwoman Pinto and members of the committee. My name is Whitney Louchheim, I am a resident of Ward 6, and I am the Co-Founder and Chief Operating Officer of Open City Advocates, which provides legal representation, holistic advocacy, and mentoring to youth who have been committed to DYRS.

When we first founded our nonprofit twenty years ago, we had a different name: Mentoring Today. From our very beginnings, we have infused mentoring best practices into our model of holistic legal representation, and we still train our attorneys with– mentoring best practices. The letter introducing the the Justice-Involved Youth in Community Act suggests that the “Trusted Adults” are intended to act as mentors, yet in the text of the bill they are empowered to judge compliance and to punish noncompliance with these agreements in a way that reads closer to “monitoring” than mentoring.

Research about mentoring best practices suggests that a punitive approach to mentoring doesn’t work, and can indeed backfire. Numerous high quality studies have identified best practices that, when followed, significantly enhance a program’s effectiveness. Some of these best practices include:

6. providing a structure through which mentors can advocate **on behalf** of their mentees.

Of all the various mediating factors that mentoring programs included, an advocacy role had by far the most significant effect on a program’s effectiveness in terms of youth outcomes.¹

It’s also important to ensure that mentoring pairs are given an opportunity to extend beyond one year, as that has been shown to be the time period over which the benefits of mentorship become concrete. Shorter periods of mentorship are not only less effective but can even be harmful.²

The Trusted Adults described in this bill appear to be perfect candidates for the kind of mentoring that turns out to be ineffective or, even worse, harmful. Assigned alongside a probation officer, with the power and responsibility to monitor compliance and punish noncompliance by sending the child into detention, this is the opposite of the advocacy role described in the best practices. The Trusted Adult “monitorship” relationship will then end abruptly, and in cases of noncompliance, in a way that lays the blame at the child’s feet—a recipe for psychological harm. We can and must do better by our young people.

We have seen this type of compliance “monitorship” before. For several years, DYRS employed “third party monitors” to track youths’ compliance with curfew and other requirements. These were not “trusted adults” in the eyes of either youth or families precisely because they were tasked with reporting on the youths’ compliance with DYRS requirements. When a trusting relationship *did* develop, some monitors would then falsify the compliance documentation in order to maintain the trusting relationship. This program was discontinued—almost certainly because it suffered from this incompatibility between being a trusted adult and simultaneously reporting on a young person’s compliance.

Thank you for the opportunity to testify regarding this bill. I’m available for questions.

### Written Testimony

#### Who We Are

Open City Advocates is a small organization with a big impact. We model an innovative solution in juvenile defense in Washington, DC, and nationwide. Founded to address the racial inequities and unjust incarceration practices in DC’s juvenile system, Open City Advocates provides youth-centered legal defense and holistic advocacy for young people after sentencing in the juvenile system.

#### Who We Serve

All of our clients have been committed to the care and custody of the DC Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services (DYRS) by DC’s Family Court. “Commitment” to DYRS means that a young person is a ward of the city, oftentimes until age 21, and that their placement may change several times with little or no court oversight—including placement at home or in a carceral facility.

There are extreme racial disparities in the youth delinquency system nationwide, with Black and Latino/a children being committed and incarcerated at 4.7 times the rate compared to their white peers.¹ It is even more extreme in the District of Columbia, where 100% of all committed children are children of color, and 96% are Black, while Black youth comprise only 51% of the DC youth population.²

Open City Advocates’ clients have had extensive contact with the delinquency system; most have also been in DC’s child welfare system, and all are at high risk for entering the DC Jail or being harmed or killed by street violence. The majority of our clients hail from DC’s Wards 7 and 8, where 35% of children live in poverty.³ Over the last two years, 46% of violent crimes committed with guns in the city occurred in these two wards.⁴ In our 20-year history, we have lost six clients to gun violence and one to a drug overdose. Open City Advocates works to help our clients envision and actualize a healthy adulthood instead of accepting death or incarceration by age 25.

#### The Justice-Involved Youth in Community Act of 2025

Good morning, Chairwoman Pinto and members of the committee. My name is Whitney Louchheim, I am a resident of Ward 6, and I am the Co-Founder and Chief Operating Officer of Open City Advocates, which provides legal representation, holistic advocacy, and mentoring to youth who have been committed to the DC Department of Youth Rehabilitation Services or DYRS.

When we first founded our nonprofit twenty years ago, we had a different name: Mentoring Today. From our very beginnings, we have infused mentoring best practices into our model of holistic legal representation, and we still train our attorneys with– mentoring best practices. The letter introducing the the Justice-Involved Youth in Community Act of 2025 suggests that the “Trusted Adults” are intended to act as mentors, yet in the text of the bill they are empowered to judge compliance and to punish noncompliance with these agreements in a way that reads closer to “monitoring” than mentoring.

Research about mentoring best practices suggests that a punitive approach to mentoring doesn’t work, and can indeed backfire. Now, to be clear, the evidence shows that mentoring for high-risk youth has a positive effect for delinquency and aggression. Indeed, Numerous high quality studies have identified best practices that, when followed, significantly enhance a program’s effectiveness. Some of these best practices include:

1. targeting youth who have already exhibited delinquent behavior,
2. serving a high proportion of males,
3. providing ongoing and timely emotional support to the mentors and mentees,
4. recruiting mentors from targeted sources to create “a good fit between the educational or occupational backgrounds of mentors and the goals of the program,”
5. matching mentoring pairs based on shared interests, and
6. providing a structure through which mentors can advocate **on behalf** of their mentees.

Importantly, programs that include more than six best practices have twice the impact of programs with fewer best practices.⁵

Mentoring programs that had an advocacy role for the mentors had effect sizes three times larger than programs that did not include an advocacy role for the mentors. Of all the various mediating factors that mentoring programs included, an advocacy role had by far the most significant effect on a program’s effectiveness in terms of youth outcomes.⁶

Over the years, our organization has incorporated all six best practices listed above. We have also implemented the fundamental best practices for mentoring, based on the National Mentoring Partnership’s Elements of Effective Practice for Mentoring, such as intensive screening of volunteers, thorough initial and in-service training, and ongoing mentor support.

In order to ensure that mentoring programs are implemented in keeping with these best practices, it’s important to train staff and mentors on service delivery and data collection regularly, using external trainers who are experts in the Positive Youth Development model, and to analyze both intermediate data like case reports and outcomes quarterly and annually to ensure compliance and validity. It’s also important to ensure that mentoring pairs are given an opportunity to extend beyond one year, as that has been shown to be the time period over which the benefits of mentorship become concrete. Shorter periods of mentorship are not only less effective but can even be harmful,⁷ and indeed it’s important that formal mentoring programs prepare participants for the eventual end of those relationships, using a closure process that ensures that youth do not feel rejection or self-blame.⁸

The Trusted Adults described in this bill appear to be perfect candidates for the kind of mentoring that turns out to be ineffective or, even worse, harmful. Assigned alongside a probation officer, with the power and responsibility to monitor compliance and punish noncompliance by sending the child into detention, this is the opposite of the advocacy role described in the best practices, not to mention the importance of voluntariness and matches based on personal interests. The Trusted Adult “monitorship” relationship will then end abruptly, and in cases of noncompliance, in a way that lays the blame at the child’s feet—a recipe for psychological harm. We can and must do better by our young people.

We have seen this type of compliance “monitorship” before. For several years around 2010-2014, DYRS employed “third party monitors” to track youths’ compliance with curfew and other requirements. These were not “trusted adults” in the eyes of either youth or families precisely because they were tasked with reporting on the youths’ compliance with DYRS requirements. When a trusting relationship *did* develop, some monitors would then falsify the compliance documentation in order to maintain the trusting relationship. This program was discontinued—almost certainly because it suffered from this incompatibility between being a trusted adult and simultaneously reporting on a young person’s compliance.

Thank you for the opportunity to testify regarding this bill. If there are any simple takeaways, it should be these: young people involved in the juvenile system benefit from mentors, but only when those mentors are matched voluntarily, have an advocacy role (the opposite of a reporting role), and when the relationship lasts more than a year. I’m available for questions.
